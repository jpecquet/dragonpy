\section{Model Description}\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Geometry and Aerodynamic Force}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{figures/model_geometry.light.png}
    \caption{Model geometry. The stroke basis $(\hat{s},\hat{n})$ is obtained
    by rotating the fixed frame basis $(\hat{x},\hat{z})$ by the stroke plane
    angle $\gamma$. The lumped wing sits at position $s(t)\,\hat{s}$ relative
    to the point mass body, with chord direction $\hat{c}$ at pitch angle
    $\psi$ from the stroke plane normal $\hat{n}$. For simplicity, only one
    lumped wing is drawn.}\label{fig:model-geometry}
\end{figure}
The flapping flight dynamics model consists of a
point mass subjected to its own weight $m\vec{g}$
and to the aerodynamic force $\vec{F}$ from $N$ wing pairs.
The point mass is free to move in the $(x,z)$ plane,
with the spatial direction $\hat{z}$ such that
$\vec{g} = -g \hat{z}$, and $\hat{x}$ such that
$\left(\hat{x},\hat{z}\right)$ forms a direct orthonormal basis.
The body position is $\vec{x} = x \hat{x} + z \hat{z}$ and
the body velocity is $\vec{u} = \dot{x} \hat{x} + \dot{z} \hat{z}$.
The total wing area is $A$, and both wings of each pair
are lumped into a single, massless surface with area $A / N$.
We will refer to these surfaces as \emph{lumped wings},
keeping in mind they each represent \emph{both} wings of a
left-right pair. The aerodynamic properties of a lumped wing
are represented by the quasi-steady model of~\cite{wang2004, wang2008}:
\begin{align}
    C_L(\alpha) &= C_{L,0} \sin 2\alpha \label{eq:cl} \\
    C_D(\alpha) &= C_{D,0} \cos^2 \alpha + C_{D,\frac{\pi}{2}} \sin^2 \alpha \label{eq:cd}
\end{align}
where $\alpha$ is the angle of attack, and the parameter values
are $C_{L,0} = 1.5$, $C_{D,0} = 0.1$, and $C_{D,\frac{\pi}{2}} = 2.0$.
The model neglects any aerodynamic interaction between lumped wings.
To express the lumped wing kinematics and aerodynamic force $\vec{F}$,
it is convenient to introduce the stroke basis $(\hat{s},\hat{n})$,
obtained by rotating the fixed frame basis $(\hat{x},\hat{z})$
by the \emph{stroke plane angle} $\gamma$. That is
\begin{align}
    \hat{s} &= \cos\gamma\hat{x} + \sin\gamma\hat{z} \\
    \hat{n} &= -\sin\gamma\hat{x} + \cos\gamma\hat{z}
\end{align}
Each lumped wing translates along the stroke axis $\hat{s}$,
and $\hat{n}$ represents the stroke plane normal.
The lumped wing position relative to the point mass body is
$\vec{s} = s(t) \hat{s}$, where $s(t)$ is the prescribed
flapping coordinate. Because the wing
is instantaneously at a distance $|s(t)|$ from the body,
the aerodynamic force acts on the body via a moment arm $|s(t)|$.
However, in the present point mass model, there is no
rotational degree of freedom, and the force is effectively
considered to act through the center of mass.

Supposing $\gamma$ stays constant during the wing
stroke, the wing velocity relative to the body is
$\vec{\dot{s}} = \dot{s}(t) \hat{s}$.
With the body velocity in the fixed frame (relative to still air) $\vec{u}$,
the wing velocity relative to the air is $\vec{v} = \vec{\dot{s}} + \vec{u}$.
The aerodynamic force for wing pair $i$ is given below (note the
subscript $i$ is omitted from quantities on the right-hand side for tidiness)
\begin{align}
    \vec{F}_i = \frac12 \rho \frac{A}{N} |\vec{v}|^2 \left[ C_L(\alpha) \hat{\ell} + C_D(\alpha) \hat{d} \right]
\end{align}
where $\rho$ is the air density and the drag direction vector $\hat{d}$
is defined as
\begin{align}
    \hat{d} = -\hat{v} = -\frac{\vec{v}}{|\vec{v}|}
\end{align}
and the lift direction vector $\hat{\ell}$ is defined as
\begin{align}
    \hat{\ell} = -\hat{d}^\perp
\end{align}
where $\cdot^\perp$ denotes counterclockwise rotation by $\pi/2$.
As a result, $(\hat{\ell},\hat{d})$ forms a direct orthonormal basis.
To express the angle of attack $\alpha$, it is convenient to
define the chord direction vector $\hat{c}$, pointing from the
trailing edge to the leading edge. Let $\psi(t)$ be the wing pitch angle,
relative to the stroke plane normal $\hat{n}$. The chord direction vector is
\begin{align}
    \hat{c} = - \sin\psi\hat{s} + \cos\psi\hat{n}
\end{align}
The geometry introduced thus far is illustrated in \autoref{fig:model-geometry}.

\subsection{Angle of Attack and Aerodynamic Coefficients}\label{sec:aoa}

The angle of attack $\alpha$ can be defined here as the
signed angle from the velocity direction vector $\hat{v}$
to the chord direction vector $\hat{c}$. We show
below that this definition is consistent
with the definition of the lift direction vector $\hat{\ell}$,
giving the correct sign for lift. With this definition,
\begin{align}
    \cos{\alpha} &= \hat{v} \cdot \hat{c} \\
    \sin{\alpha} &= \hat{v} \times \hat{c}
\end{align}
where $\hat{v} = \vec{v} / |\vec{v}|$ and $\times$
denotes the 2D cross product, defined as $\vec{a}\times\vec{b} = a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1$.
The velocity composition, the angle of attack, and the resulting
lift and drag are illustrated in \autoref{fig:velocity-triangle}.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{figures/velocity_triangle.light.png}
    \caption{Lumped wing velocity triangle and aerodynamic force.
    The wing velocity relative to the air, $\vec{v} = \vec{\dot{s}} + \vec{u}$,
    composes the flapping velocity $\vec{\dot{s}} = \dot{s}\,\hat{s}$ (along
    the stroke axis, dotted) with the body velocity $\vec{u}$. The angle of
    attack $\alpha$ is the signed angle from $\hat{v}$ to the chord direction
    $\hat{c}$, with $\hat{p} = \hat{c}^\perp$ the chord-normal direction,
    and the lift and drag contributions are drawn along
    $\hat{\ell}$ and $\hat{d}$ with lengths proportional to $C_L$ and $C_D$
    (here $0 < \alpha < \frac{\pi}{2}$, so
    $C_L > 0$).}\label{fig:velocity-triangle}
\end{figure}
$C_L(\alpha)$ can be rewritten as a function of
$\cos \alpha$ and $\sin \alpha$ only; indeed,
\begin{align}
    C_L(\alpha) &= C_{L,0} \sin 2\alpha \nonumber \\
                &= 2 C_{L,0} \sin \alpha \cos \alpha
\end{align}
$C_L$ and $C_D$ can then be expressed with simple vector math as follows
\begin{align}
    C_L &= 2 C_{L,0} (\hat{v} \cdot \hat{c}) (\hat{v} \times \hat{c}) \\
    C_D &= C_{D,0} (\hat{v} \cdot \hat{c})^2 + C_{D,\frac{\pi}{2}} (\hat{v} \times \hat{c})^2
\end{align}
Introducing the chord-normal direction $\hat{p} = \hat{c}^\perp$ and
the wing velocity components along and normal to the chord,
$v_c = \vec{v} \cdot \hat{c}$ and $v_p = \vec{v} \cdot \hat{p}$,
we have $\hat{v} \cdot \hat{c} = v_c / |\vec{v}|$,
$\hat{v} \times \hat{c} = -v_p / |\vec{v}|$, and
$|\vec{v}|^2 = v_c^2 + v_p^2$, so the coefficients expand to the
scalar form
\begin{align}
    C_L &= -2 C_{L,0} \frac{v_c\, v_p}{v_c^2 + v_p^2} \\
    C_D &= \frac{C_{D,0}\, v_c^2 + C_{D,\frac{\pi}{2}}\, v_p^2}{v_c^2 + v_p^2}
\end{align}
This treatment of $C_L$ is the same as that of \cite{andersen2005},
but with the opposite sign convention, $\alpha$ being defined
from $\hat{c}$ to $\hat{v}$ in that work, whereas
it is defined from $\hat{v}$ to $\hat{c}$ here.
The lift and drag directions resulting from the above expressions
are checked for correctness in all four quadrants of the angle of attack in
\autoref{fig:liftdrag-quadrants}.
Because $\hat{\ell}$ and $\hat{d}$ are constructed from
$\hat{v}$ alone, the wing attitude enters only through $C_L$ and $C_D$,
so it suffices to check the coefficient signs
quadrant by quadrant. Naturally, drag is positive along $\hat{d}$ for every
$\alpha$. The sign of $C_L$ is that of
$-v_c v_p$, which is that of $\sin2\alpha$: positive in quadrants I and III, negative
in II and IV, as drawn.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{figures/liftdrag_quadrants.light.png}
    \caption{Lift and drag directions in the four quadrants of the angle of
    attack $\alpha$, measured from the wing velocity direction $\hat{v}$ to
    the chord direction $\hat{c}$. Panels are labeled by quadrant
    (I--IV). Drag ($C_D > 0$) always opposes the wing
    velocity along $\hat{d}$, while the sign of $C_L$
    orients lift along $\pm\hat{\ell}$. The hollow circle marks the
    wing leading edge (the $\hat{c}$ end of the
    chord).}\label{fig:liftdrag-quadrants}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Prescribed Wing Kinematics}

Wing motion is prescribed by the flapping coordinate $s(t)$
and the pitch angle $\psi(t)$. Flapping motion for each lumped
wing is taken to be sinusoidal:
\begin{align}
    s(t) &= s_0 \cos(\omega t + \phi)
\end{align}
where $s_0$ and $\omega$ are set parameters for all wing pairs,
and $\phi$ is a phase parameter specific to a given wing pair,
allowing for out-of-phase flapping of distinct wing pairs.
For pitch, we consider two different kinematics. The first is
sinusoidal:
\begin{align}
    \psi(t) &= \psi_0 + \psi_1 \cos(\omega t + \phi - \delta_0)
\end{align}
where $\psi_0$ (mean pitch), $\psi_1$ (pitch amplitude),
and $\delta_0$ (pitch phase lead) are set parameters
for all wing pairs. The second kinematics law is simpler: through each
half of the wing stroke, the wing pitch remains constant.
The half-stroke along $-\hat{s}$
corresponds to $\omega t + \phi \in [0,\,\pi[\pmod{2\pi}$,
and the half-stroke along $+\hat{s}$
to $\omega t + \phi \in [\pi,\,2\pi[\pmod{2\pi}$.
The simplified pitch kinematics are, then,
\begin{align}
    \psi(t) =
    \begin{cases}
        \psi_0 + \psi_1 & \text{if } \omega t + \phi \in [0,\,\pi[\pmod{2\pi} \\
        \psi_0 - \psi_1 & \text{if } \omega t + \phi \in [\pi,\,2\pi[\pmod{2\pi}
   \end{cases}
\end{align}
This can be considered an approximation of
the sinusoidal law with $\delta_0 = \frac{\pi}{2}$.
Both pitch kinematics laws are illustrated in
\autoref{fig:pitch-kinematics}. As the figure makes apparent,
$\delta_0 = \frac{\pi}{2}$ keeps the leading edge oriented into
the wing motion throughout the cycle, with pitch extremes at
mid-stroke and $\psi = \psi_0$ at stroke reversal.
$\delta_0 = -\frac{\pi}{2}$ produces the opposite, adverse
orientation, while $\delta_0 = 0$ and $\pi$ place the pitch
extremes at the reversals.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{figures/pitch_kinematics.light.png}
    \caption{Wing motion through the two halves of the flapping cycle, for the
    sinusoidal pitch kinematics with
    $\delta_0 \in \{0, \frac{\pi}{2}, -\frac{\pi}{2}, \pi\}$ and for
    the constant-pitch simplification. Across all panels, $\psi_0 = 0$ and $\psi_1 = 45^\circ$.
    The top row shows the half-stroke along $+\hat{s}$
    (with $\hat{s}$ pointing right) and the bottom row
    the half-stroke along $-\hat{s}$. Arrows indicate the direction
    of motion, the hollow circle marks the wing leading edge, and snapshots
    are taken at equal time increments, crowding near the stroke reversals
    where the flapping motion slows. The constant-pitch kinematics match
    the $\delta_0 = \pi/2$ kinematics well, except near stroke reversal.}\label{fig:pitch-kinematics}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Equation of Motion and Nondimensionalization}

The point mass is subjected to its own weight
and to the aerodynamic force from each wing pair, so the equation of motion is
\begin{align}
    m \frac{d^2 \vec{x}}{d t^2} &= m \vec{g} + \frac12 \rho A \sum_{i=1}^N \frac1N |\vec{v}_i|^2 \left[ C_{L,i} \hat{\ell}_i + C_{D,i} \hat{d}_i \right]
\end{align}
The equation can be nondimensionalized using
the body length $L$ as the length scale, and the
gravitational time scale $\sqrt{L/g}$,
giving an `acceleration scale' $g$ and
a `velocity scale' $\sqrt{gL}$.
Introducing the $*$ superscript to denote nondimensionalized
quantities, the equation of motion can be divided by $mg$
and becomes
\begin{align}
    \ddot{x}^* \hat{x} + \ddot{z}^* \hat{z} &= -\hat{z} + \frac12 \frac{\rho A L}{m} \frac1N \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{gL} |\vec{v}_i|^2 \left[ C_{L,i} \hat{\ell}_i + C_{D,i} \hat{d}_i \right] \nonumber \\
                                            &= -\hat{z} + \frac12 \frac{\rho A L}{m} \frac1N \sum_{i=1}^N |\vec{v}_i^*|^2 \left[ C_{L,i} \hat{\ell}_i + C_{D,i} \hat{d}_i \right]
\end{align}
in which (dropping the $i$ subscript for conciseness)
\begin{align}
    \vec{v}^* = \frac{1}{\sqrt{gL}} \left( \vec{u} + \vec{\dot{s}} \right)
\end{align}
We introduce the dimensionless inverse wing loading $A^*/m^*$
defined as
\begin{align}
    \frac{A^*}{m^*} = \frac{\rho A L}{m} 
\end{align}
giving the final form of the dimensionless equation of motion
\begin{align}
    \ddot{x}^* \hat{x} + (\ddot{z}^* + 1) \hat{z} &= \frac12 \frac{A^*}{m^*} \frac1N \sum_{i=1}^N |\vec{v}_i^*|^2 \left[ C_{L,i} \hat{\ell}_i + C_{D,i} \hat{d}_i \right]
\end{align}
The wing kinematics themselves must also be nondimensionalized.
Introducing $\tau^* = \omega t$ and $s_0^* = s_0 / L$,
\begin{align}
    s^*(\tau^*) &= s_0^* \cos(\tau^* + \phi) \\
    \psi(\tau^*) &= \psi_0 + \psi_1 \cos(\tau^* + \phi - \delta_0)
\end{align}
Finally, the dimensionless wing velocity along $\hat{s}$ relative to the body is
\begin{align}
    \dot{s}^*(\tau^*) &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{gL}} \frac{d}{dt} s_0 \cos(\omega t + \phi) \nonumber \\
                      &= -\frac{1}{\sqrt{gL}} s_0 \omega \sin(\tau^* + \phi) \nonumber \\
                      &= - s_0^* \omega^* \sin(\tau^* + \phi)
\end{align}
with $\omega^* = \omega \sqrt{L/g}$. The dimensionless
velocities can be used straightforwardly in place
of the dimensional velocities in the expressions of \autoref{sec:aoa}
to obtain $C_L$ and $C_D$. In the following,
we deal exclusively with the dimensionless quantities.

\section{Controller Design}\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Problem Statement}\label{sec:problem-statement}

We wish to design a control strategy to achieve
arbitrary trajectories in the two-dimensional plane,
including prey pursuit trajectories. We assume that
the flyer's own state is known using a variety
of sensory inputs, so position and velocity are
available as inputs to the controller. In addition,
when a prey is present in the environment,
the bearing angle to the prey, as detected by the eyes,
is also an available input. Because we are
particularly interested in \emph{dragonfly}
flight, we make two design decisions such that the resulting
flight characteristics resemble those of a dragonfly.
First, hovering should be achieved with an inclined stroke
plane (tilted from the horizontal), characterized by
the parameter $\gamma^\text{hover}$. Second, when at speed, the
stroke-plane normal should be approximately aligned
with the direction of travel, as observed in experiments
\cite{azuma1985}. This last choice will simplify
the analysis to follow, since any flow velocity
component parallel to the stroke plane can be neglected.
With these choices in mind, the contents of this section
otherwise hold for flapping flight in general, as we
do not assume specific morphological characteristics
like number of wing pairs, size, etc.

\subsection{Mean Aerodynamic Force}\label{sec:maf}

\subsubsection{Definitions and Assumptions}

To affect its trajectory, the flying body
must modulate and direct the aerodynamic force
acting on its wings. Because the aerodynamic force
on a flapping wing is by nature highly unsteady,
it is convenient to consider the wingbeat-cycle average
of the aerodynamic force, summed over each of the wings,
which we call the \emph{mean aerodynamic force}, $\langle \vec{F}^* \rangle$.
Throughout, $\langle \cdot \rangle$ denotes the average over one
wingbeat cycle,
$\langle f \rangle = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^{2\pi} f \, d\tau^*$,
evaluated at fixed body velocity.
In reality, body velocity is not constant over a wingbeat cycle: there
are (a) oscillations inherent to flapping, and there may be
(b) a net change in body velocity between the start and end of the cycle.
Assuming the oscillations are small compared to the characteristic
wing velocity, and that any body velocity changes
occur at a slow rate relative to the wingbeat period, the body
velocity may be considered constant over a wingbeat cycle.
The following assumes these conditions are met.
Our objective is now to understand how $\langle \vec{F}^* \rangle$
depends on the wing kinematics at any given body velocity,
so that we are able to formulate a control strategy.

As explained in \autoref{sec:problem-statement}, we
are designing a controller under the assumption that the body velocity
is normal to the stroke plane. Beyond morphology and kinematics,
then, there is a single body velocity parameter $\langle u^* \rangle$,
which is the cycle-averaged body velocity magnitude (the direction
being set to the stroke-plane normal $\hat{n}$).
It is convenient to introduce a similarity parameter in
the form of the \emph{advance ratio}, $J$, which is the
ratio of the cycle-averaged body velocity to the
characteristic wing velocity:
\begin{align}
    J = \frac{\langle u^* \rangle}{s_0^* \omega^*}
\end{align}
The instantaneous wing velocity $\vec{v}^*$
relative to the air has the stroke-plane normal and
in-plane components
\begin{align}
    v_n^* &= \langle u^* \rangle = s_0^* \omega^* J \\
    v_s^* &= -s_0^* \omega^* \sin\tau^*
\end{align}
with $\tau^*$ as introduced in \autoref{sec:model},
and the $+\hat{s}$ half-stroke corresponds to $\sin\tau^* < 0$.
Note that the phase term $\phi$ is dropped. Indeed each wing pair
carries the same area fraction $1/N$ and differs from the others only by
its phase $\phi$, which shifts its force history within the cycle
without changing the cycle average at fixed body velocity. The
per-pair averages are therefore identical, the sum over pairs and
the $1/N$ factors cancel, and $\langle \vec{F}^* \rangle$ is
computed below as for a single lumped wing of total area $A$, with
$\phi$ dropped. Phase differences do shape the
\emph{instantaneous} force, which is smoother
when the lumped wings beat out of phase, but not the mean force.
The squared norm of the velocity is
\begin{align}
    (v^*)^2 &= \left(s_0^* \omega^*\right)^2 \left[ J^2 + \sin^2\tau^* \right]\label{eq:v_sq}
\end{align}
We introduce the signed angle $\chi$ from
$\hat{n}$ to $\vec{v}^*$, defined as
\begin{align}
    \chi = -\tan^{-1} \frac{v_s^*}{v_n^*} = \tan^{-1} \frac{\sin\tau^*}{J}
\end{align}
Both $\psi$ and $\chi$ are signed angles measured from $\hat{n}$ with
the same sense (positive tilting counterclockwise, toward $-\hat{s}$).
They locate the chord direction $\hat{c}$, and the velocity direction $\hat{v}$, respectively.
The angle of attack as defined in \autoref{sec:model} (the
signed angle from $\hat{v}$ to $\hat{c}$) is therefore simply
\begin{align}
    \alpha = \psi - \chi = \psi - \tan^{-1} \frac{\sin\tau^*}{J} \label{eq:alpha}
\end{align}
The stroke-frame velocity decomposition and the angles $\psi$, $\chi$,
and $\alpha$ are illustrated in \autoref{fig:flow-angles}.
\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{figures/flow_angles.light.png}
    \caption{Stroke-frame velocity decomposition and flow angle. With the
    body velocity assumed normal to the stroke plane, the instantaneous wing
    velocity $\vec{v}^*$ composes the in-plane flapping component
    $v_s^*\,\hat{s}$ with the normal component $\langle u^* \rangle\,\hat{n}$.
    The pitch angle $\psi$ and the flow angle $\chi$ are measured from
    $\hat{n}$ with the same signed sense (positive tilting toward
    $-\hat{s}$), locating the chord direction $\hat{c}$ and the velocity
    direction $\hat{v}$, so the angle of attack is $\alpha = \psi - \chi$.
    Drawn on the $+\hat{s}$ half-stroke ($\sin\tau^* < 0$), where both
    $\psi$ and $\chi$ are negative.}\label{fig:flow-angles}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Hover Case $(J=0)$}

The mean aerodynamic force derivation is
somewhat involved in the general case,
so we first present the hovering case, which
corresponds to $J = 0$.
With $J=0$, the flow angle saturates at
$\chi = \frac{\pi}{2} \frac{\sin\tau^*}{|\sin\tau^*|}$
and the angle of attack simplifies to
\begin{align}
    \alpha = \psi - \frac{\pi}{2} \frac{\sin\tau^*}{|\sin\tau^*|}\label{eq:alpha-hover}
\end{align}
Using the appropriate trigonometric identities, the
aerodynamic coefficients (defined in \autoref{eq:cl} and \autoref{eq:cd})
are
\begin{align}
    C_L &= -C_{L,0} \sin 2\psi \label{eq:cl-hover} \\
    C_D &= \frac{C_{D,\frac{\pi}{2}} + C_{D,0}}{2} + \frac{C_{D,\frac{\pi}{2}} - C_{D,0}}{2} \cos 2\psi \label{eq:cd-hover}
\end{align}
both independent of the stroke direction; the direction dependence
resides entirely in the force direction vectors.
Because the wing velocity relative to the air
is purely in-plane (along $\hat{s}$), the in-plane aerodynamic force
component is purely drag, and the out-of-plane
component (along $\hat{n}$) is purely lift: the force directions are
$\hat{d} = \frac{\sin\tau^*}{|\sin\tau^*|} \hat{s}$ and
$\hat{\ell} = -\hat{d}^\perp = -\frac{\sin\tau^*}{|\sin\tau^*|} \hat{n}$,
alternating with the stroke direction, as shown in \autoref{fig:hover-quadrants}.
\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{figures/hover_quadrants.light.png}
    \caption{Lift and drag directions over the hover wingbeat ($J=0$), for
    the four sign combinations of the pitch angle $\psi$ and the
    stroke phase $\sin\tau^*$, drawn in the stroke frame. The wing
    velocity $\vec{v}^*$ is purely in-plane, so drag is purely in-plane,
    and lift is purely out-of-plane. 
    The hollow circle marks the leading edge. The pitch
    angle $\psi$, measured from $\hat{n}$, and the angle of attack $\alpha$
    of \autoref{eq:alpha-hover}, measured from $\hat{v}$, are marked as in
    \autoref{fig:flow-angles}. $\alpha$ quadrants I--IV
    are marked as in \autoref{fig:liftdrag-quadrants}. The ``useful''
    quadrants (lift along $\hat{n}$) are I and IV.}\label{fig:hover-quadrants}
\end{figure}
The mean aerodynamic force components are
\begin{align}
    \langle F_n^* \rangle &= -\frac12 \frac{A^*}{m^*} \left( s_0^* \omega^* \right)^2 \langle C_L \sin \tau^* |\sin \tau^*| \rangle \nonumber \\
    &= \frac12 \frac{A^*}{m^*} \left( s_0^* \omega^* \right)^2 C_{L,0} \langle \sin 2\psi\; \sin \tau^* |\sin \tau^*| \rangle \\
    \langle F_s^* \rangle &= \frac12 \frac{A^*}{m^*} \left( s_0^* \omega^* \right)^2 \langle C_D \sin \tau^* |\sin \tau^*| \rangle \nonumber \\
    &= \frac12 \frac{A^*}{m^*} \left( s_0^* \omega^* \right)^2 \frac{C_{D,\frac\pi2} - C_{D,0}}{2}\, \langle \cos 2\psi\; \sin \tau^* |\sin \tau^*| \rangle
\end{align}
Note the constant drag term vanishes because
$\langle \sin \tau^* |\sin \tau^*| \rangle = 0$.
We introduce the shorthand $\Delta C_D = C_{D,\frac\pi2} - C_{D,0}$.
The expressions feature a common pre-factor
we call $q^*$, defined as
\begin{align}
    q^* = \frac14 \frac{A^*}{m^*} \left( s_0^* \omega^* \right)^2\label{eq:q}
\end{align}
We further introduce the normal and in-plane force
coefficients $C_{F^*_n}$ and $C_{F^*_s}$, defined as
\begin{align}
    C_{F^*_n} &= \frac{\langle F^*_n \rangle}{q^*} = 2 C_{L,0} \langle \sin 2\psi\; \sin \tau^* |\sin \tau^*| \rangle \label{eq:cfn} \\
    C_{F^*_s} &= \frac{\langle F^*_s \rangle}{q^*} = \Delta C_D \langle \cos 2\psi\; \sin \tau^* |\sin \tau^*| \rangle \label{eq:cfs}
\end{align}
which we synthesize into the overall force magnitude coefficient
\begin{align}
    C_{F^*} = \sqrt{C_{F^*_n}^2 + C_{F^*_s}^2}
\end{align}
This coefficient is useful because it is only
a function of the pitch kinematic parameters and the lift
and drag coefficient parameters, and is independent
of morphology and wingbeat frequency and amplitude, which appear
only in the pre-factor $q^*$. 
By maximizing the value of this coefficient,
given a constraint on the force direction
required to achieve a given maneuver,
the characteristic wingbeat velocity $s_0^* \omega^*$ can be minimized.
With our parametrization
of the pitch kinematics, and with the lift
and drag coefficient parameters set, $C_{F^*}$
and its $(n,s)$ decomposition are functions
of 3 variables: $\psi_0$, $\psi_1$, and $\delta_0$.
We visualize $C_{F^*}$, $C_{F^*_n}$, and $C_{F^*_s}$
across slices of this 3D space in \autoref{fig:cf_components}
and \autoref{fig:cf}.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{figures/cf_components.light.png}
    \caption{(a) Stroke-plane-normal (lift) component $C_{F^*_n}$ and
    (b) in-plane (drag) component $C_{F^*_s}$ of the force coefficient
    over the pitch plane at $\delta_0 = 90^\circ$. The force direction
quadrant in the $(\hat{s},\hat{n})$ plane can be inferred from the signs.}\label{fig:cf_components}
\end{figure}
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{figures/pitch_efficiency.light.png}
    \caption{Dependence of $C_{F^*}$ on pitch parameters at zero
        body velocity over (a) the $(\delta_0,\psi_1)$ plane
        at $\psi_0 = 0$ and (b) the $(\psi_0, \psi_1)$ plane
        at $\delta_0 = 90^\circ$.
        Maximum shown as a white dot on both panels.}\label{fig:cf}
\end{figure}

As panel (a) of \autoref{fig:cf} makes evident,
significantly more force is produced when
$\delta_0 = \pm90^\circ$. Because $\delta_0 = -90^\circ$
results in the mean force pointing along $-\hat{n}$
when the mean pitch is neutral ($\psi_0 = 0^\circ$),
we select $\delta_0 = +90^\circ$ as the operating point.
This choice maximizes the mean force for a given
$(\psi_0, \psi_1)$, and reduces the pitch parameter space to 2D.
How then do we select $\psi_0$ and $\psi_1$? It is
clear from panel (b) that a certain value of $\psi_1$
maximizes $C_{F^*}$ for any given $\psi_0$.
We will call it $\psi_1^\text{opt}$.
In addition, $\psi_0 = 0^\circ\pmod{90^\circ}$
maximizes $C_{F^*}$. Looking at panel (b) of \autoref{fig:cf_components},
it is clear that the in-plane force component is zero when
$\psi_0 = 0^\circ\pmod{90^\circ}$, so the force
is purely normal to the stroke plane. In addition,
from panel (a) of the same figure, we see that $\psi_0 = 0^\circ$
results in the mean force pointing towards $+\hat{n}$,
while $\psi_0 = \pm90^\circ$ results in the mean force
pointing towards $-\hat{n}$. We select neutral pitch
($\psi_0 = 0^\circ$) as the reference,
for consistency with the choice of $\delta_0 = 90^\circ$.
In summary, the reference pitch kinematics
\begin{align}
    \psi_0 = 0^\circ \quad\quad\quad \psi_1 = \psi_1^\text{opt} \quad\quad\quad \delta_0 = 90^\circ
\end{align}
result in $C_{F^*} = C_{F^*}^\text{max}$,
and $\langle \vec{F}^* \rangle$
pointing towards $+\hat{n}$.

These reference kinematics allow for \emph{efficient} hover
(in the sense that the characteristic wingbeat velocity $s_0^* \omega^*$
is minimized, because $C_{F^*}$ is maximized) when the stroke
plane is horizontal ($\gamma^\text{hover} = 0^\circ$). Indeed, since
the mean force vector points towards $+\hat{n}$,
$+\hat{n}$ must be vertical and opposed to the direction
of gravity. To model dragonfly hover specifically,
at an inclined stroke plane ($\gamma^\text{hover} \neq 0^\circ$),
the mean force vector must have an in-plane component.
Looking at panel (b) of \autoref{fig:cf_components},
it is clear that this can be achieved by changing $\psi_0$,
away from the symmetric $0^\circ$ point. For convenience,
we introduce $\beta$, the signed angle from the stroke-plane
normal $\hat{n}$ to the mean force vector. Since the mean force is
$\langle \vec{F}^* \rangle = q^* \left( C_{F^*_n} \hat{n} + C_{F^*_s} \hat{s} \right)$,
$\beta$ can be expressed from the $C_{F^*_n}$ and $C_{F^*_s}$ coefficients
(\autoref{eq:cfn} and \autoref{eq:cfs}) as
\begin{align}
    \beta = -\tan^{-1} \frac{C_{F^*_s}}{C_{F^*_n}}
    = -\tan^{-1} \left[ \frac{\Delta C_D}{2 C_{L,0}}
    \frac{\langle \cos2\psi \sin\tau^* |\sin\tau^*| \rangle}{\langle \sin2\psi \sin\tau^* |\sin\tau^*| \rangle} \right]\label{eq:beta}
\end{align}
$\beta$ carries the same signed sense as $\psi$ and $\chi$
(positive counterclockwise, from $\hat{n}$ toward $-\hat{s}$),
hence the minus sign (see \autoref{fig:beta-def}).
\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{figures/beta_definition.light.png}
    \caption{Definition of the mean-force direction angle $\beta$.
    The mean force
    $\langle \vec{F}^* \rangle = q^* \left( C_{F^*_n} \hat{n} +
    C_{F^*_s} \hat{s} \right)$ is decomposed into its stroke-frame
    components (\autoref{eq:cfn}, \autoref{eq:cfs}). Here the positive
    mean pitch gives $C_{F^*_s} < 0$, so the force tilts
    counterclockwise off the normal and $\beta$ is positive. The wing is
    drawn over one full wingbeat.}\label{fig:beta-def}
\end{figure}
We can simplify the $\langle \cos2\psi \sin\tau^* |\sin\tau^*| \rangle / \langle \sin2\psi \sin\tau^* |\sin\tau^*| \rangle$
ratio in the above expression.
With $\delta_0 = 90^\circ$, the pitch kinematics reduce to
$\psi = \psi_0 + \psi_1 \sin\tau^*$. Using the appropriate
trigonometric identities,
\begin{align}
    \cos 2\psi &= \cos 2\psi_0 \cos\left(2\psi_1 \sin\tau^*\right) - \sin 2\psi_0 \sin\left(2\psi_1 \sin\tau^*\right) \\
    \sin 2\psi &= \sin 2\psi_0 \cos\left(2\psi_1 \sin\tau^*\right) + \cos 2\psi_0 \sin\left(2\psi_1 \sin\tau^*\right)
\end{align}
Under the half-period shift $\tau^* \to \tau^* + \pi$, the factors
$\sin\tau^* |\sin\tau^*|$ and $\sin\left(2\psi_1 \sin\tau^*\right)$
change sign while $\cos\left(2\psi_1 \sin\tau^*\right)$ is unchanged,
so the $\cos\left(2\psi_1 \sin\tau^*\right)$ terms average to zero,
leading to
\begin{align}
    \langle \cos2\psi\; \sin\tau^* |\sin\tau^*| \rangle &= -\sin 2\psi_0\, \langle \sin\left(2\psi_1 \sin\tau^*\right) \sin\tau^* |\sin\tau^*| \rangle \\
    \langle \sin2\psi\; \sin\tau^* |\sin\tau^*| \rangle &= \cos 2\psi_0\, \langle \sin\left(2\psi_1 \sin\tau^*\right) \sin\tau^* |\sin\tau^*| \rangle
\end{align}
The common $\langle \sin\left(2\psi_1 \sin\tau^*\right) \sin\tau^* |\sin\tau^*| \rangle$
factor cancels out in the ratio, giving simply
\begin{align}
    \frac{\langle \cos2\psi\; \sin\tau^* |\sin\tau^*| \rangle}{\langle \sin2\psi\; \sin\tau^* |\sin\tau^*| \rangle} = -\tan 2\psi_0
\end{align}
and \autoref{eq:beta} becomes
\begin{align}
    \beta = \tan^{-1} \left[ \frac{\Delta C_D}{2 C_{L,0}} \tan 2\psi_0 \right]\label{eq:beta-hover}
\end{align}
Importantly, $\beta$ is independent of $\psi_1$,
and is only controlled by $\psi_0$. It follows that
$\psi_0$ is an appropriate control variable for
the mean force direction relative to the stroke-plane
normal $\hat{n}$. $\beta$ is shown as a function
of $\psi_0$ in \autoref{fig:beta_vs_psi0}.
As discussed above, $C_{F^*}$ becomes less than
its maximum when $\psi_0 \neq 0^\circ\pmod{90^\circ}$, away
from the reference pitch kinematics, so it is also shown
in addition to $\beta$.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{figures/force_direction_test.light.png}
    \caption{Effect of $\psi_0$ on force direction angle $\beta$ and force coefficient $C_{F^*}$.}\label{fig:beta_vs_psi0}
\end{figure}
In the $\Delta C_D / \left( 2 C_{L,0} \right) \to 1$ limit,
the relation between $\beta$ and $\psi_0$
can be approximated as $\beta \approx 2\psi_0$.
This holds only roughly here because 
$\Delta C_D / \left( 2 C_{L,0} \right) = 1.9/3 \approx 0.63$,
but the trend is the same, as can be seen in the figure.
To achieve hover at an inclined stroke plane
with tilt angle $\gamma^\text{hover}$, the mean force vector
angle must be $\beta = -\gamma^\text{hover}$. 
This requires $\psi_0 = \psi_0^\text{hover}$, given
by \autoref{eq:beta-hover} (specifically the inverse
relation) when $\beta = -\gamma^\text{hover}$.
The two hover configurations are illustrated in
\autoref{fig:hover-beta-gamma}.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{figures/hover_beta_gamma.light.png}
    \caption{Hover at (a) a horizontal stroke plane and (b) an inclined
    stroke plane (drawn here with $\gamma < 0$). The wing is drawn over one full wingbeat at
    equal time increments, with the $-\hat{s}$ half-stroke behind, in
    darker grey, and the $+\hat{s}$ half-stroke in front. In (a), the
    reference pitch kinematics ($\psi_0 = 0^\circ$) set the mean force
    $\langle \vec{F}^* \rangle$ vertically along $\hat{n}$.
    In (b), the mean force remains vertical while $\hat{n}$
    is tilted because $\psi_0^\text{hover}$ is trimmed to bias
    $\langle \vec{F}^* \rangle$ by $\beta = -\gamma^\text{hover}$
    using the inverse of \autoref{eq:beta-hover}.}\label{fig:hover-beta-gamma}
\end{figure}

Now that we have narrowed down the pitch
kinematics for hover, we step back to consider
the flapping kinematics as well, governed by
parameters $s_0^*$ (amplitude) and $\omega^*$
(frequency). The condition for hover on the mean
force magnitude is
\begin{align}
    C_{F^*} q^* = 1 \implies (s_0^* \omega^*)^2 = \frac{4}{C_{F^*} \frac{A^*}{m^*}}
\end{align}
We prefer to consider $\omega^*$ as a fixed
parameter rather than a control variable,
leaving $s_0^*$ as the primary control for
the force amplitude. The hover value is given by
\begin{align}
    (s_0^*)^\text{hover} = \frac{1}{\omega^*} \sqrt{\frac{4}{C_{F^*}^\text{hover} \frac{A^*}{m^*}}}
\end{align}
The hover kinematic parameters are summarized in \autoref{tab:hover}.
The control variables are $s_0^*$ and $\psi_0$.

\subsubsection{General Case $(J \geq 0)$}

For $J \neq 0$, the wing velocity relative to the air is no longer
purely in-plane, so lift and drag each contribute to both force
components. In terms of the angle $\chi$, the wing velocity direction
is $\hat{v} = \cos\chi\, \hat{n} - \sin\chi\, \hat{s}$. Drag acts
along $-\hat{v}$, and lift acts along the perpendicular direction
\begin{align}
    \hat{\ell} = -\hat{d}^\perp = -\cos\chi\, \hat{s} - \sin\chi\, \hat{n}
\end{align}
recovering the hover directions at $\chi = \pm\frac{\pi}{2}$.
The instantaneous force is
\begin{align}
    \vec{F}^* = \frac12 \frac{A^*}{m^*} (v^*)^2 \left( C_L\, \hat{\ell} - C_D\, \hat{v} \right)
\end{align}
with components
\begin{align}
    F^*_n &= -\frac12 \frac{A^*}{m^*} (v^*)^2 \left( \sin\chi\; C_L + \cos\chi\; C_D \right) \\
    F^*_s &= -\frac12 \frac{A^*}{m^*} (v^*)^2 \left( \cos\chi\; C_L - \sin\chi\; C_D \right)
\end{align}
Substituting $\cos\chi = J/\sqrt{J^2 + \sin^2\tau^*}$,
$\sin\chi = \sin\tau^*/\sqrt{J^2 + \sin^2\tau^*}$, and the expression
for $(v^*)^2$ (\autoref{eq:v_sq}), we get
\begin{align}
    F^*_n &= -\frac12 \frac{A^*}{m^*} \left( s_0^* \omega^* \right)^2 \sqrt{J^2 + \sin^2\tau^*}\, \left( \sin\tau^*\; C_L + J\; C_D \right) \\
    F^*_s &= -\frac12 \frac{A^*}{m^*} \left( s_0^* \omega^* \right)^2 \sqrt{J^2 + \sin^2\tau^*}\, \left( J\; C_L - \sin\tau^*\; C_D \right)
\end{align}
From $\alpha = \psi - \chi$ (\autoref{eq:alpha}), the aerodynamic
coefficients (\autoref{eq:cl} and \autoref{eq:cd}) expand as
\begin{align}
    C_L &= C_{L,0} \left( \cos2\chi\; \sin2\psi - \sin2\chi\; \cos2\psi \right) \\
    C_D &= \bar{C}_D - \frac{\Delta C_D}{2} \left( \cos2\chi\; \cos2\psi + \sin2\chi\; \sin2\psi \right)
\end{align}
where we define $\bar{C}_D \equiv \left( C_{D,\frac\pi2} + C_{D,0} \right)/2$
for brevity, and where the double angles are smooth functions of
the wing phase:
\begin{align}
    \cos 2\chi = \frac{J^2 - \sin^2\tau^*}{J^2 + \sin^2\tau^*}
    \quad\quad\quad
    \sin 2\chi = \frac{2 J \sin\tau^*}{J^2 + \sin^2\tau^*}
\end{align}
Substituting into the force components and averaging, the force
coefficients are
\begin{align}
    C_{F^*_n} &= -2 \left\langle \frac{\left[ C_{L,0} \left( J^2 - \sin^2\tau^* \right) - \Delta C_D\, J^2 \right] \sin\tau^*\, \sin2\psi}{\sqrt{J^2 + \sin^2\tau^*}} \right\rangle \nonumber \\
    &\quad + 2 \left\langle \frac{J \left[ 2 C_{L,0} \sin^2\tau^* + \frac{\Delta C_D}{2} \left( J^2 - \sin^2\tau^* \right) \right] \cos2\psi}{\sqrt{J^2 + \sin^2\tau^*}} \right\rangle
    - 2 J \bar{C}_D \left\langle \sqrt{J^2 + \sin^2\tau^*} \right\rangle \label{eq:cfn-general} \\
    C_{F^*_s} &= -2 \left\langle \frac{J \left[ C_{L,0} \left( J^2 - \sin^2\tau^* \right) + \Delta C_D \sin^2\tau^* \right] \sin2\psi}{\sqrt{J^2 + \sin^2\tau^*}} \right\rangle \nonumber \\
    &\quad + 2 \left\langle \frac{\left[ 2 C_{L,0} J^2 - \frac{\Delta C_D}{2} \left( J^2 - \sin^2\tau^* \right) \right] \sin\tau^*\, \cos2\psi}{\sqrt{J^2 + \sin^2\tau^*}} \right\rangle \label{eq:cfs-general}
\end{align}
Note the constant ($\bar{C}_D$) drag term of $C_{F^*_s}$ vanishes
because $\langle \sin\tau^* \sqrt{J^2 + \sin^2\tau^*} \rangle = 0$.
Setting $J = 0$ recovers \autoref{eq:cfn} and \autoref{eq:cfs},
as expected. At the operating point $\delta_0 = 90^\circ$,
$\psi = \psi_0 + \psi_1 \sin\tau^*$, and the parity argument of the
hover case applies unchanged: under $\tau^* \to \tau^* + \pi$, the
bracketed polynomials and $\sqrt{J^2 + \sin^2\tau^*}$ are invariant,
so expanding $\sin2\psi$ and $\cos2\psi$ and discarding the vanishing
averages leaves
\begin{align}
    C_{F^*_n} &= 2\, I_n(J, \psi_1)\, \cos2\psi_0 - 2 J \bar{C}_D\, I_0(J) \label{eq:cfn-general-op} \\
    C_{F^*_s} &= -2\, I_s(J, \psi_1)\, \sin2\psi_0 \label{eq:cfs-general-op}
\end{align}
with
\begin{align}
    I_0 &= \left\langle \sqrt{J^2 + \sin^2\tau^*} \right\rangle \\
    I_n &= \left\langle \frac{J \left[ 2 C_{L,0} \sin^2\tau^* + \frac{\Delta C_D}{2} \left( J^2 - \sin^2\tau^* \right) \right] \cos\left( 2\psi_1 \sin\tau^* \right)}{\sqrt{J^2 + \sin^2\tau^*}} \right\rangle \nonumber \\
    &\quad - \left\langle \frac{\left[ C_{L,0} \left( J^2 - \sin^2\tau^* \right) - \Delta C_D\, J^2 \right] \sin\tau^*\, \sin\left( 2\psi_1 \sin\tau^* \right)}{\sqrt{J^2 + \sin^2\tau^*}} \right\rangle \\
    I_s &= \left\langle \frac{J \left[ C_{L,0} \left( J^2 - \sin^2\tau^* \right) + \Delta C_D \sin^2\tau^* \right] \cos\left( 2\psi_1 \sin\tau^* \right)}{\sqrt{J^2 + \sin^2\tau^*}} \right\rangle \nonumber \\
    &\quad + \left\langle \frac{\left[ 2 C_{L,0} J^2 - \frac{\Delta C_D}{2} \left( J^2 - \sin^2\tau^* \right) \right] \sin\tau^*\, \sin\left( 2\psi_1 \sin\tau^* \right)}{\sqrt{J^2 + \sin^2\tau^*}} \right\rangle
\end{align}
The angle from the stroke-plane normal to the mean force
(\autoref{eq:beta}) becomes
\begin{align}
    \beta = \tan^{-1} \left[ \frac{I_s \sin2\psi_0}{I_n \cos2\psi_0 - J \bar{C}_D\, I_0} \right] \label{eq:beta-general}
\end{align}
In the limit $J \to 0$, the $I_0$ term vanishes and
\begin{align}
    I_n \to C_{L,0} \left\langle \sin\left( 2\psi_1 \sin\tau^* \right) \sin\tau^* |\sin\tau^*| \right\rangle
    \quad\quad\quad
    I_s \to \frac{\Delta C_D}{2} \left\langle \sin\left( 2\psi_1 \sin\tau^* \right) \sin\tau^* |\sin\tau^*| \right\rangle
\end{align}
recovering \autoref{eq:beta-hover}. The overall force
coefficient $C_{F^*}$ is shown in \autoref{fig:cf_J}
for values of $J$ from 0 to 1, at $\delta_0 = 90^\circ$.
Highlighted on each panel
is the reference point from the hover plot \autoref{fig:cf},
tracked as it turns from a global maximum at $J=0$ to a local
maximum, and eventually to a saddle point.
This point represents the maximum
force available in the $+\hat{n}$ direction for a given $J$.
The region of space within the dotted contour around
the reference point corresponds to $C_{F^*_n} > 0$.
Within this region, the stroke-plane-normal
component of the mean force points towards $+\hat{n}$.

The values of $C_{F^*}$ and $\psi_1^\text{opt}$ at the reference point

As the figure makes clear, $\psi_1^\text{opt}(J)$ decreases as $J$ increases.
Recall that $\alpha = \psi - \chi$ and consider the stroke half

Mid-stroke, when $\sin\tau^* = \mp 1$, $\chi = \mp \tan^{-1} (1/J)$

trend for $\psi_1^\text{opt}(J)$:
\begin{align}
    \psi_1^\text{opt}(J) \approx \psi_1^\text{opt}(0) - \tan^{-1} J
\end{align}
which is also shown in \autoref{fig:hover_opt_J}, and
does appear to be the leading order effect.

\subsubsection{Constant-Pitch Simplification}\label{sec:square-wave}

The general-case results above are exact but not closed-form:
$\psi_1$ remains inside the cycle averages through the
$\cos\left(2\psi_1\sin\tau^*\right)$ and
$\sin\left(2\psi_1\sin\tau^*\right)$ factors, so quantities
such as $\psi_1^\text{opt}(J)$ and the maps of
\autoref{fig:cf_J} must be evaluated numerically. Closed
forms can be obtained by replacing the sinusoidal pitch
schedule with the constant-pitch idealization of
\autoref{sec:model}, restated here in terms of the stroke-direction
sign $\sigma \equiv -\sin\tau^*/|\sin\tau^*|$ ($\sigma = +1$ on the
$+\hat{s}$ half-stroke):
\begin{align}
    \psi(\tau^*) = \psi_0 - \sigma\, \psi_1 \label{eq:psi-square}
\end{align}
which holds the pitch constant at $\psi_0 - \psi_1$ during the
forward half-stroke ($\sigma = +1$) and at $\psi_0 + \psi_1$
during the backward half-stroke ($\sigma = -1$). The pitch flip
occurs at stroke reversal, since we impose $\delta_0 = 90^\circ$.

This implies instantaneous rotation of the wing, which is not
achievable by a real wing but is mostly harmless in the quasi-steady
model because the in-plane wing velocity vanishes at reversal.
The flip produces at most a bounded force discontinuity due to the
normal inflow, when $J > 0$.
The simulations in the remainder of this report retain
the sinusoidal schedule; the constant-pitch law serves purely as
an analytical surrogate.

Since $\sigma^2 = 1$, the pitch trigonometric factors are
piecewise constant over each half-stroke:
\begin{align}
    \sin 2\psi &= \sin 2\psi_0 \cos 2\psi_1 - \sigma \cos 2\psi_0 \sin 2\psi_1 \\
    \cos 2\psi &= \cos 2\psi_0 \cos 2\psi_1 + \sigma \sin 2\psi_0 \sin 2\psi_1
\end{align}
Comparing with the expansions used in the hover case, the
constant-pitch law simply amounts to the substitutions
\begin{align}
    \cos\left(2\psi_1 \sin\tau^*\right) \to \cos 2\psi_1
    \quad\quad\quad
    \sin\left(2\psi_1 \sin\tau^*\right) \to -\sigma \sin 2\psi_1 \label{eq:square-subs}
\end{align}
Since $\sigma$ changes sign under the half-period shift
$\tau^* \to \tau^* + \pi$, exactly like the
$\sin\left(2\psi_1\sin\tau^*\right)$ factor it replaces, every
vanishing average identified previously still vanishes, and the
pitch dependence now factors entirely out of the remaining
averages.

In the hover case, applying the substitutions to \autoref{eq:cfn}
and \autoref{eq:cfs}, and using
$\langle \sigma \sin\tau^* |\sin\tau^*| \rangle = -\langle \sin^2\tau^* \rangle = -\frac12$,
gives
\begin{align}
    C_{F^*_n} = C_{L,0} \sin 2\psi_1 \cos 2\psi_0
    \quad\quad\quad
    C_{F^*_s} = -\frac{\Delta C_D}{2} \sin 2\psi_1 \sin 2\psi_0 \label{eq:cf-hover-square}
\end{align}
and therefore
\begin{align}
    C_{F^*} = \sin 2\psi_1 \sqrt{C_{L,0}^2 \cos^2 2\psi_0 + \frac{\Delta C_D^2}{4} \sin^2 2\psi_0}
\end{align}
The structure observed numerically in \autoref{fig:cf_components}
and \autoref{fig:cf} is now explicit. The component ratio
$C_{F^*_s} / C_{F^*_n} = -\left( \Delta C_D / 2 C_{L,0} \right) \tan 2\psi_0$
reproduces \autoref{eq:beta-hover} identically---as it must,
since that relation was derived independently of the $\psi_1$
schedule. The pitch amplitude enters only through the
$\sin 2\psi_1$ prefactor, so the optimum is exact and independent
of $\psi_0$:
\begin{align}
    \psi_1^\text{opt} = 45^\circ
    \quad\quad\quad
    C_{F^*}^\text{max} = C_{L,0}
\end{align}
the latter attained at the reference $\psi_0 = 0^\circ$ (since
$C_{L,0} > \Delta C_D / 2$). The maximum has a transparent
interpretation: with $\psi_1 = 45^\circ$ and $\psi_0 = 0^\circ$,
the angle of attack is $\alpha = \psi + \sigma \frac\pi2 = \sigma\, 45^\circ$,
i.e.\ $|\alpha| = 45^\circ$
on both half-strokes---the constant-pitch law holds the wing at the
lift-maximizing angle of attack for the entire cycle, so the
force coefficient equals $C_{L,0}$ exactly. The sinusoidal
schedule only passes through this ideal angle twice per
half-stroke, which is why its optimum is displaced
($\psi_1^\text{opt} \approx 51^\circ$) and slightly weaker
($C_{F^*}^\text{max} \approx 1.43$ versus $C_{L,0} = 1.5$, a
$5\%$ overestimate by the constant-pitch law).

In the general case, the same substitutions pull $\psi_1$ out of
$I_n$ and $I_s$:
\begin{align}
    I_n = A_n \cos 2\psi_1 + B_n \sin 2\psi_1
    \quad\quad\quad
    I_s = A_s \cos 2\psi_1 + B_s \sin 2\psi_1 \label{eq:In-Is-square}
\end{align}
where $A_n$, $B_n$, $A_s$, and $B_s$ are functions of $J$ only.
Introducing the family of averages
\begin{align}
    W_k(J) = \left\langle \frac{|\sin\tau^*|^k}{\sqrt{J^2 + \sin^2\tau^*}} \right\rangle
    \quad\quad\quad
    k \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}
\end{align}
they read
\begin{align}
    A_n &= J \left[ \left( 2 C_{L,0} - \frac{\Delta C_D}{2} \right) W_2 + \frac{\Delta C_D}{2} J^2 W_0 \right]
    &
    A_s &= J \left[ C_{L,0} J^2 W_0 - \left( C_{L,0} - \Delta C_D \right) W_2 \right] \\
    B_n &= C_{L,0} W_3 - \left( C_{L,0} - \Delta C_D \right) J^2 W_1
    &
    B_s &= \frac{\Delta C_D}{2} W_3 + \left( 2 C_{L,0} - \frac{\Delta C_D}{2} \right) J^2 W_1
\end{align}
The odd-$k$ members are elementary,
\begin{align}
    W_1 = \frac{2}{\pi} \tan^{-1} \frac{1}{J}
    \quad\quad\quad
    W_3 = \frac{1}{\pi} \left[ \left( 1 - J^2 \right) \tan^{-1} \frac{1}{J} + J \right]
\end{align}
while the even-$k$ members, together with $I_0$, are complete
elliptic integrals: with $\mu = 1 / \left( 1 + J^2 \right)$,
\begin{align}
    W_0 = \frac{2}{\pi} \sqrt{\mu}\, K(\mu)
    \quad\quad\quad
    W_2 = I_0 - J^2 W_0
    \quad\quad\quad
    I_0 = \frac{2}{\pi} \frac{E(\mu)}{\sqrt{\mu}}
\end{align}
where $K(\mu) = \int_0^{\pi/2} \left( 1 - \mu \sin^2\theta \right)^{-1/2} d\theta$
and $E(\mu) = \int_0^{\pi/2} \left( 1 - \mu \sin^2\theta \right)^{1/2} d\theta$.
\autoref{eq:cfn-general-op}, \autoref{eq:cfs-general-op}, and
\autoref{eq:beta-general} then hold unchanged with these $I_n$
and $I_s$, so the mean aerodynamic force is closed-form in all
of the kinematic parameters. Setting $J = 0$ gives
$A_n = A_s = 0$, $B_n = C_{L,0}/2$, and $B_s = \Delta C_D / 4$,
recovering \autoref{eq:cf-hover-square}.

Along the reference axis $\psi_0 = 0^\circ$,
\autoref{eq:cfn-general-op} becomes a pure harmonic in $2\psi_1$,
\begin{align}
    C_{F^*_n} = 2 \left( A_n \cos 2\psi_1 + B_n \sin 2\psi_1 \right) - 2 J \bar{C}_D\, I_0
\end{align}
so the continued hover optimum tracked numerically in
\autoref{fig:hover_opt_J} becomes exact:
\begin{align}
    \psi_1^\text{opt}(J) = \frac12 \tan^{-1} \frac{B_n}{A_n}
    \quad\quad\quad
    \left( C_{F^*_n} \right)^\text{max} = 2 \sqrt{A_n^2 + B_n^2} - 2 J \bar{C}_D\, I_0 \label{eq:psi1-opt-square}
\end{align}
At $J = 0$, $A_n = 0$ and $\psi_1^\text{opt} = 45^\circ$; as $J$
grows, $A_n$ increases from zero (both $A_n$ and $B_n$ are
positive here), so $\psi_1^\text{opt}$ decreases monotonically.
The closed form thus reproduces, and explains, the trend of
\autoref{fig:hover_opt_J}: the growing $A_n \cos 2\psi_1$ term,
which carries the normal-inflow contributions and peaks at
$\psi_1 = 0^\circ$, pulls the optimum toward smaller pitch
amplitudes---exactly the mechanism captured by the flow-tracking
approximation drawn dashed in
\autoref{fig:hover_opt_J}. \autoref{tab:square-wave} compares
the constant-pitch predictions with the sinusoidal schedule:
$\psi_1^\text{opt}$ is offset low by $5$--$6^\circ$, and the
available force is captured to within $5\%$ across $J \in [0,1]$
(an overestimate at hover, a slight underestimate at speed).

Beyond its transparency, this limit has practical value for
control. The trim of the two control variables
$\left( s_0^*, \psi_0 \right)$ to a demanded mean
force---performed numerically in
\autoref{sec:control-law}---becomes analytically invertible: for
a demanded force direction, \autoref{eq:beta-general} reduces to
a harmonic equation of the form
$a \sin 2\psi_0 + b \cos 2\psi_0 = c$ with known coefficients,
yielding $\psi_0$ explicitly, after which $s_0^*$ follows from
the demanded force magnitude through $q^*$ (\autoref{eq:q}). The
constant-pitch expressions could therefore serve as an analytical
initialization---or replacement---for the numerical trim solver,
at the cost of the few-percent model error quantified in
\autoref{tab:square-wave}.

\subsection{Control Strategy}\label{sec:strategy}

From the above analysis, we propose a control strategy.
It will be discussed qualitatively in this section,
and formalized in the following section (\autoref{sec:control-law}).
We begin by going over \emph{what to do} with each of the
wing kinematic parameters.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Stroke plane angle $\gamma$:} when hovering,
        $\gamma = \gamma^\text{hover}$. When moving, $\gamma$
        should change to effectively obtain, or at least
        approximate, the axial inflow we have assumed in the analysis.
        There is the issue of the transition between these
        two phases, which may be abrupt if the commanded
        flight direction is not aligned with the stroke-plane normal
        at hover. We propose that $\gamma$ be varied smoothly from
        its hover value to its moving flight value based on $J$.
    \item \emph{Stroke amplitude $s_0^*$:} primary control
        for the mean force magnitude, should be varied so that
        the force magnitude demand is met. In hovering or in general steady flight,
        this means having the mean force magnitude equal the weight.
    \item \emph{Mean pitch $\psi_0$:} primary control
        for the mean force direction relative to the stroke-plane
        normal, should be varied so that the force direction demand
        is met. In hovering or in general steady flight,
        this means having the mean force vector point toward $+\hat{z}$.
    \item \emph{Pitch amplitude $\psi_1$:} should be varied with
        $J$ to maximize the available force, as shown in \autoref{fig:hover_opt_J}.
    \item \emph{Pitch phase $\delta_0$:} fixed to $90^\circ$, not a control variable.
    \item \emph{Wingbeat frequency $\omega^*$:} constant parameter, not a control variable.
\end{itemize}
In summary, the two variables that directly act to
modulate and direct the force are $s_0^*$ and $\psi_0$.
The two remaining variables, $\gamma$ and $\psi_1$,
adapt to the velocity vector to respectively
approximate an axial inflow, and maximize the
available force.

A comment should be made here about the $\hat{x}$-wise
asymmetry introduced by the nonzero $\gamma^\text{hover}$.
At low speeds, when the stroke plane is still close to
its hover orientation, this will result in different
behaviors for motion toward $+\hat{x}$ and $-\hat{x}$.
To restore symmetry, we propose to flip the sign
of $\gamma^\text{hover}$ when the commanded flight
direction changes.

\subsection{Control Law}\label{sec:control-law}

We now formalize the strategy of \autoref{sec:strategy}.
Let $\vec{x}^*$ and $\vec{u}^*$ be the body position and
velocity.
The force demand is formed by a velocity tracking law:
given a reference velocity $\vec{u}^*_r$, the desired
acceleration is proportional to the velocity error, through a
single gain $K$, and the weight is compensated:
\begin{align}
    \vec{a}^*_\text{des} &= K \left( \vec{u}^*_r - \vec{u}^* \right) \\
    \langle \vec{F}^* \rangle^\text{des} &= \vec{a}^*_\text{des} + \hat{z} \label{eq:fdes}
\end{align}
No position or acceleration terms are used: the task enters
the law only through $\vec{u}^*_r$. (A previous version of
this report discusses augmenting \autoref{eq:fdes} with
position feedback and acceleration feedforward terms to
provide positional accuracy; we do not pursue this here.)
The gain $K$ has a simple closed-loop reading: whenever the trim
described below delivers the demanded mean force, the
cycle-averaged equation of motion reduces to
$\langle \dot{\vec{u}}^* \rangle = K \left( \vec{u}^*_r -
\vec{u}^* \right)$, first-order velocity tracking with time
constant $1/K$. Two effects bound the gain from above. First,
the law is actuated once per wingbeat, with the kinematic
parameters held over each cycle, so the loop is discrete: over
one wingbeat period $T^* = 2\pi/\omega^*$, the velocity error is
multiplied by approximately $\left( 1 - K T^* \right)$, giving
stability for $K T^* < 2$ and one-beat convergence at
$K T^* = 1$. Second, a large gain turns routine velocity errors
into force demands outside the reachable force set, saturating
the trim. Within these ceilings the choice is free; the
simulations of \autoref{sec:results} all use $K = 2.7$, slightly
above the one-beat gain of the reference configuration
($K T^* \approx 1.2$).
The reference velocity defines the use case:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Hover:} $\vec{u}^*_r = \vec{0}$. The law nulls
        the body velocity, and the body hovers about wherever
        it settles; no reference position is involved.
    \item \emph{Prescribed trajectory:} the trajectory is
        specified as a path traversed with a speed schedule,
        and $\vec{u}^*_r(t)$ is the velocity of the reference
        point moving along it---the unit tangent of the path,
        scaled by the scheduled speed. The path geometry
        enters the law only through this velocity history.
    \item \emph{Prey pursuit:} the reference velocity is the
        desired closing speed along the line of sight to the
        prey,
        \begin{align}
            \vec{u}^*_r = u^*_\text{cmd}\, \hat{r}
            \quad\quad\quad
            \hat{r} = \frac{\vec{x}^*_\text{prey} - \vec{x}^*}{|\vec{x}^*_\text{prey} - \vec{x}^*|}
            \label{eq:ur-pursuit}
        \end{align}
        formed from the line-of-sight bearing only, with no
        range information. The law then steers the velocity
        vector onto the line of sight as the body closes in.
\end{itemize}

\autoref{fig:pursuit-diagram}.

With the force demand formulated, $\gamma$ and $\psi_1$ 
must now adapt to the measured velocity.
The advance ratio is evaluated as
\begin{align}
    J = \frac{|\vec{u}^*|}{\left(s_0^* \omega^*\right)^\text{ref}}
\end{align}
where $\left(s_0^* \omega^*\right)^\text{ref}$ is the
wingbeat velocity of the reference configuration at the hover condition, held fixed so
that $J$ does not depend on the control variable $s_0^*$.
This $J$ is formed from the speed alone, regardless of the
velocity direction relative to the stroke plane, and serves to
parameterize the schedules; the trim below sees the full
velocity vector.
The stroke-plane angle blends from the inclined-hover value to
the velocity-aligned value $\gamma_u$, defined such that
$\hat{n}$ is aligned with $\vec{u}^*$---the two-argument
arctangent
$\gamma_u = \operatorname{atan2} \left( -u^*_x,\, u^*_z \right)$,
giving $\mp 90^\circ$ in level flight toward $\pm\hat{x}$,
$0^\circ$ in climb, and $\pm 180^\circ$ in descent:
\begin{align}
    \gamma = \left[ 1 - f(J) \right] \sigma_x \gamma^\text{hover}
    + f(J)\, \gamma_u
    \quad\quad\quad
    f(J) = 1 - e^{-(J/J_c)^2} \label{eq:gamma-schedule}
\end{align}
with $J_c = 0.35$, placed between the two velocity regimes the
schedule must separate: residual station-keeping velocities
($J \lesssim 0.1$) engage the blend by under a tenth
($f < 0.08$), keeping the stroke plane at its hover lean, while
at cruise speeds ($J \gtrsim 2 J_c$) the blend is essentially
complete ($f > 0.98$). The sign $\sigma_x \in \{-1, +1\}$ sets the
lean of the hover stroke plane (\autoref{sec:strategy}): it is
the sign of the horizontal component of the commanded velocity,
$\sigma_x = \operatorname{sgn} \left( \vec{u}^*_r \cdot \hat{x}
\right)$, latched---retaining its previous value whenever
$\vec{u}^*_r \cdot \hat{x} = 0$---and initialized to $+1$.
Latching on the commanded rather than the measured velocity
keeps the residual drift of station-keeping from flipping the
stroke plane.
\autoref{fig:gamma-schedule-level} illustrates the schedule in
level flight: the stroke plane holds its hover lean near rest,
and is nearly velocity-aligned once $J$ exceeds roughly $2 J_c$.

    as the forward speed builds (middle, right), the blend leans

The schedule is applied unrestricted. In descent, $\gamma_u$
aims the stroke-plane normal downward, along the fall, and
weight support then requires a mean force along $-\hat{n}$: as
established in \autoref{sec:maf} (\autoref{fig:cf_J}), such a
reversed force is reached at mean pitch near $\pm 90^\circ$,
where drag from the normal inflow dominates, and the trim
formulated below (\autoref{eq:trim}) steers there. This is the
delicate regime of the scheme---fast descents saturate the trim
(\autoref{sec:results})---and it motivates the alternate gliding
descent mode discussed in the conclusion.
The pitch amplitude is slaved to the force-maximizing value of
\autoref{fig:hover_opt_J},
\begin{align}
    \psi_1 = \psi_1^\text{opt}(J)
    \label{eq:psi1-slave}
\end{align}
under an assumed axial inflow. The actual inflow deviates from
the stroke-plane normal during transients, but only appreciably
at low advance ratio, where the optimum is insensitive to the
inflow direction, so the deviation is neglected.
With $(\gamma, \psi_1)$ now set, the remaining two variables
$(s_0^*,\psi_0)$
are trimmed to the force demand by solving
\begin{align}
    \langle \vec{F}^* \rangle \left( s_0^*, \psi_0;\,
    \gamma, \psi_1, \vec{u}^* \right)
    = \langle \vec{F}^* \rangle^\text{des} \label{eq:trim}
\end{align}
for $(s_0^*, \psi_0)$. Care must be taken with which force map
is inverted here. In \autoref{eq:trim},
$\langle \vec{F}^* \rangle$ is the cycle average of the
instantaneous force law of \autoref{sec:model}, evaluated at the
\emph{measured} velocity $\vec{u}^*$ with no assumption on its
direction; the trim therefore remains exact during transients,
when the inflow is oblique to the stroke plane. The axial-inflow
expressions of \autoref{sec:maf} coincide with this map when
$\hat{n}$ is aligned with $\vec{u}^*$---the condition the
$\gamma$ schedule works to restore---and they expose its
structure.
The two unknowns match the two numbers the planar demand supplies
once resolved in the stroke frame: a direction, the signed angle
$\beta^\text{des}$ from the stroke-plane normal $\hat{n}$ to
$\langle \vec{F}^* \rangle^\text{des}$, and a magnitude
$F^\text{des} = | \langle \vec{F}^* \rangle^\text{des} |$, both
known once $\gamma$ is set. The mean force at the operating point,
$\langle \vec{F}^* \rangle = q^* \left( C_{F^*_n} \hat{n}
+ C_{F^*_s} \hat{s} \right)$ with the coefficients of
\autoref{eq:cfn-general-op} and \autoref{eq:cfs-general-op},
splits these two roles cleanly between the two unknowns. Because
$J$ is evaluated at the fixed reference wingbeat velocity, the
coefficients are independent of $s_0^*$, which enters only through
the flapping dynamic pressure $q^* \propto (s_0^* \omega^*)^2$
(\autoref{eq:q}): $s_0^*$ scales the mean force without turning
it. The direction $\beta$ is in turn governed by $\psi_0$ alone
(\autoref{eq:beta-general}), while the magnitude depends on both
unknowns. \autoref{eq:trim} is therefore triangular, equivalent
to the pair
\begin{align}
    \beta \left( \psi_0;\, \psi_1, J \right) = \beta^\text{des}
    \quad\quad\quad
    q^*\, C_{F^*} \left( \psi_0;\, \psi_1, J \right) = F^\text{des}
    \label{eq:trim-split}
\end{align}
solved in that order: the direction demand fixes $\psi_0$ by
inverting \autoref{eq:beta-general}, and the magnitude demand then
yields the amplitude explicitly through $q^*$ (\autoref{eq:q}),
\begin{align}
    s_0^* = \frac{1}{\omega^*}
    \sqrt{\frac{4 F^\text{des}}{C_{F^*} \frac{A^*}{m^*}}}
\end{align}
with $C_{F^*}$ evaluated at the trimmed pitch. This is the
demand-driven generalization of the hover trim of
\autoref{sec:maf}, recovered when the body is at rest and the
demand reduces to the weight: $\beta^\text{des} =
-\gamma^\text{hover}$ returns $\psi_0^\text{hover}$, and
$F^\text{des} = 1$ returns $(s_0^*)^\text{hover}$.

In practice, the once-per-wingbeat actuation matches the
cycle-averaged nature of \autoref{eq:trim}, and we solve the
coupled form rather than the split \autoref{eq:trim-split}:
at the measured velocity, the inflow is generally oblique to the
stroke plane, and the advance ratio experienced by the wing
varies with $s_0^*$, re-coupling the pair beyond the axial,
frozen-$J$ picture. A damped Newton iteration on
the residual of \autoref{eq:trim}, with a finite-difference
Jacobian and iterates confined to the admissible kinematic ranges,
converges in a handful of steps: the near-triangular structure
keeps the system well-conditioned, and each solve is warm-started
from the previous wingbeat's $(s_0^*, \psi_0)$, so the demand
moves little between solves. In the constant-pitch limit the split
becomes fully explicit and can initialize---or replace---the
iteration, as noted in \autoref{sec:square-wave}. When the demand
leaves the reachable force set, the iteration saturates at the
kinematic bounds and the demand is met only approximately; this is
the trim saturation encountered in the steep descents of
\autoref{sec:results}.

Finally, once the commanded reference velocity vanishes
(hover, or an exhausted reference trajectory) and the body has
settled near rest ($J < 0.05$ in our simulations),
$\gamma$ and $\psi_1$ are pinned to their hover values
$\sigma_x \gamma^\text{hover}$ and $\psi_1^\text{opt}(0)$,
avoiding jitter from the residual hover velocity, while
\autoref{eq:trim} continues to hold hover equilibrium. The pin
is seamless: at this threshold the blend is engaged by about
$2\%$, and both schedules sit within a few degrees of their
hover values.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Reference Configuration}

To ground our results, we introduce a
reference configuration based on morphological
and wingbeat frequency data for \emph{Anisoptera}
from~\cite{wakeling1997} and~\cite{aracheloff2024},
respectively. Nondimensionalized, the data range
is [0.2, 1.0] for $A^* / m^*$, and [8, 20] for $\omega^*$.
For the reference configuration, we select the intermediate values
\begin{align}
    \frac{A^*}{m^*} = 0.6 \quad\quad\quad\quad\quad \omega^* = 14
\end{align}
In addition, we choose the hovering stroke plane angle to be
\begin{align}
    \gamma^\text{hover} = -45^\circ
\end{align}
defined with a negative sign (clockwise) so that it corresponds to
a horizontal dragonfly pointing towards $+\hat{x}$.
When settling from lateral motion towards $-\hat{x}$,
the hovering stroke plane angle would be $-\gamma^\text{hover} = +45^\circ$,
as explained in \autoref{sec:strategy}.

Since dragonflies have two wing pairs, we set $N=2$,
with the two pairs flapping in antiphase (their phase parameters
$\phi$ differing by $180^\circ$) so as to reduce body oscillations.

\subsection{Hover}

The hover regime of the control law is exercised by initializing
the body at the origin with a velocity perturbation
$\vec{u}^*(0) = (0.2, 0.2)$, and $\vec{u}^*_r = 0$, with
$K = 2.7$. The response is shown in \autoref{fig:hover}.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{figures/hover_gains.light.png}
    \caption{Hover hold under the velocity law
    (\autoref{eq:fdes}, $K = 2.7$). The velocity perturbation is
    eliminated within about one time unit. The position offset
    accumulated meanwhile (about $0.05$ body lengths) is never
    corrected---there is no position feedback---and some slow
    residual drift remains, but the body settles into a clean
    hover about the settled point.}\label{fig:hover}
\end{figure}

The sensitivity to the single gain is checked by repeating this
recovery across a sweep of gains, $K T^* \in [0.2, 2.7]$
(\autoref{fig:gain-sweep}). Below the stability boundary, the
discrete model of \autoref{sec:control-law} holds well: the
measured per-beat contraction of the velocity error follows
$1 - K T^*$, with a slightly milder slope---at these gains the
velocity changes appreciably \emph{within} a beat, weakening the
realized braking relative to the frozen-velocity trim---which
places the measured one-beat gain near $K T^* \approx 1.1$
rather than $1$. The settling time is U-shaped with its minimum
there, staying under one time unit for $K T^*$ between roughly
$1$ and $1.3$, and the accumulated drift follows the discrete
prediction $|\vec{u}^*(0)| \left( 1/K - T^*/2 \right)$. The
loop remains stable out to $K T^* \approx 2.1$, just past the
predicted bound of $2$. Two findings round out the picture.
First, the trim never saturates across the entire stable range:
for a perturbation of this size, the reachable-force ceiling is
not the binding constraint. Second, past the stability boundary
the growing errors do saturate the trim, and the saturation
clamps the divergence into a bounded wandering of a few body
lengths rather than an escape. The gain used throughout,
$K = 2.7$ ($K T^* \approx 1.2$), sits in the flat basin around
the measured one-beat optimum---settling in two wingbeats, with
nearly a factor of two of gain margin.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{figures/gain_sweep.light.png}
    \caption{Gain sensitivity of the hover recovery of
    \autoref{fig:hover}, swept over $K T^*$ (gain $\times$
    wingbeat period). Open circle: the gain $K = 2.7$ used
    throughout; dotted line: the one-beat gain $K T^* = 1$;
    shaded band: beyond the predicted stability bound
    $K T^* = 2$. (a) Per-beat contraction of the velocity error
    (least-squares fit of $\vec{e}_{n+1} = r\, \vec{e}_n$ over

    $1 - K T^*$; in the unstable band the fit reflects bounded
    wandering, not contraction. (b) Time for the beat-sampled
    error to settle below $5\%$ of the perturbation; the last

    offset, against the discrete prediction
    $|\vec{u}^*(0)| \left( 1/K - T^*/2 \right)$; unstable gains

\subsection{Prescribed Trajectory}

The reference path is a closed-course test trajectory covering
several flight regimes: a level start easing into a $45^\circ$
climb, a tight self-crossing loop (radius $0.4$), a vertical
descent of nearly three body lengths, two sharp corners (radius
$0.25$) framing a level run, and a gentle quarter-turn (radius
$1$) onto a level finish. The course is assembled from straight
lines and tangent circular arcs, with all control vertices on the
integer grid, and is traversed with a trapezoidal speed schedule
(linear ramps from and to rest over a distance of $0.3$, cruise
speed $0.5$), which together define $\vec{u}^*_r(t)$. The cruise
speed is kept moderate because the vertical descent is the
demanding segment: at faster descent rates the trim saturates
(see the descent caveat in the conclusion). Starting from rest on
the path, the tracking response with $K = 2.7$ is shown in
\autoref{fig:traj}.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{figures/trajectory_gains.light.png}

    settles next to the course endpoint (final offset

\end{figure}

the recorded drawing---$19$ body lengths of continuous turns through

into the lower wing lobes are steeper than the descent of the course
above and saturate the trim at the same cruise speed, so it is flown
at a reduced cruise of $0.4$.

\section{Conclusion}

The proposed control scheme is capable of
achieving hover and prey pursuit, and of
following arbitrary trajectories to
a reasonable degree of accuracy.
Some caveats are worth mentioning:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Not strictly proportional control}. While
    all control tasks can be achieved with a single
    proportional gain $K$ in the force demand formulation,
    the control variables vary nonlinearly with
    the force demand, since we are solving the inverse
    problem of which variable values satisfy the force demand.
    Following the hover controller presented in
    the previous report, we may be able to resolve this
    by linearizing the force, using the expressions
    we derived in \autoref{sec:maf}.
\item \emph{Issue with descent}. When descending (towards $-\hat{z}$)
    at a fast rate, the stroke-plane normal will point downwards.
    However, to achieve a steady descent velocity, the force vector must
    point up, opposed to gravity. The force vector, then,
    must point toward $-\hat{n}$. This can be achieved
    as we saw in \autoref{sec:maf} with large enough $|\psi_0|$,
    but at high $J$ it results in large force magnitudes
    which lead to instability in the unsteady dynamics,
    and prevent clean descent trajectories.
    This could potentially be addressed with an
    alternate control mode using gliding instead of
    flapping to achieve steady descent.
    Note that descent is not as problematic if the commanded
    trajectory has a sustained downward acceleration
    larger than that due to gravity,
    since the requirement that the force oppose gravity
    does not hold.
\end{itemize}
