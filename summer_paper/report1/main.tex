\section{Model Description}%
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Summary}

We model a dragonfly-like insect with two pairs of wings.
The sagittal plane is aligned with the fixed frame $(x,z)$
plane, where $z$ points opposite the direction of gravity.
The wings in each pair beat symmetrically about the sagittal
plane, and there is zero initial velocity or wind component
normal to the sagittal plane, so the motion is constrained
to the $(x,z)$ plane, rendering the problem two-dimensional.
A subsequent strong simplification is that the body is reduced
to a point mass, so there is no coupling between body pitch
and wing kinematics. In this manner we can focus entirely on
the translational dynamics without concern for flight stability.
The dynamical model is nondimensionalized using the body length $L$,
the body mass $m$, and the gravitational time scale $\sqrt{L/g}$.
The resulting dimensionless parameters, along with the wing kinematics
angular parameters, are listed in \autoref{tab:nondim_params}.
Dimensionless quantities are denoted by an asterisk ($*$).

\subsection{Aerodynamic Force}

\subsubsection{Model and Derivation}

Each wing is modeled as a single, massless plate element of
area $A_w$ with its aerodynamic center at the $\frac23-$span station,
$r = \frac23 L_w$, whose
lift and drag coefficients are prescribed by
functions of the instantaneous angle of attack $\alpha$.
These simplified functions, given by \autoref{eq:cl}
and \autoref{eq:cd}, nevertheless capture the essential phenomena.
Parameters for the wing kinematics are defined in \autoref{fig:kinematics1}
and \autoref{fig:kinematics2}
for a single right-hand-side wing. Since the wings of each pair
are assumed to beat symmetrically about the sagittal plane,
the left-hand-side wing kinematics can be obtained by mirroring about
the $(x,z)$ plane.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=5in]{../figures/kinematics1.png}
    \caption{Wing stroke geometry}\label{fig:kinematics1}
\end{figure}

Each wing element sweeps along an arc of radius $r = \frac23 L_w$
that lies inside the stroke plane, with normal vector
$\vec{n} = \cos \gamma \vec{z} - \sin \gamma \vec{x}$,
where $\gamma$ is the stroke plane inclination angle
relative to the vertical. The sweep angle $\phi$ about $\vec{n}$
is defined relative to the side vector $\vec{y}$, positive
counterclockwise about $\vec{n}$.
At any point along the wing stroke, the wing velocity
relative to the body is
\begin{align}
    \vec{v}_{w/b} = \frac23 L_w \dot{\phi} \vec{s}
\end{align}
with $\vec{s} = \cos\phi \left( \cos \gamma \vec{x} + \sin \gamma \vec{z} \right) -\sin\phi \vec{y}$.
The sweep angle $\phi$ for wing pair $i$ is prescribed by
\begin{align}
    \phi^{(i)}(t) = \phi_1 \sin \left(\omega t - \sigma_0^{(i)}\right)
\end{align}
The phase parameter $\sigma_0^{(i)}$ is set to $0$ for
the forewing pair, and to $\sigma_0$ for the hindwing pair.
Both wing pairs beat at the same frequency $\omega$ and
with the same amplitude $\phi_1$. In addition to the sweeping
motion, the wing rotates about its own spanwise axis.
We refer to this as the wing pitch. It is most easily
described in the $(\vec{s},\vec{n})$ plane.
\autoref{fig:kinematics2} shows the pitch angle $\psi$
defined relative to the stroke plane normal $\vec{n}$.
$\psi$ is prescribed for wing pair $i$ by
\begin{align}
    \psi^{(i)}(t) = \psi_0 + \psi_1 \sin \left(\omega t - \delta_0 - \sigma_0^{(i)}\right)
\end{align}
Unlike for the sweep angle $\phi$, the mean pitch angle $\psi_0$
may be nonzero, and can be used to redirect the mean aerodynamic
force vector, as we will see.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=6in]{../figures/kinematics2.png}
    \caption{Wing pitch geometry and angle of attack for (a) $\vec{v}_w\cdot\vec{s} > \vec{c}\cdot\vec{s}$ and (b) $\vec{v}_w\cdot\vec{s} < \vec{c}\cdot\vec{s}$}\label{fig:kinematics2}
\end{figure}

It is convenient to define the chord direction vector
$\vec{c} = \cos\psi \vec{n} - \sin\psi \vec{s}$.
The angle of attack $\alpha$ is the angle between
$\vec{c}$ and $\vec{v}_w$, the projection in the
$(\vec{s},\vec{n})$ plane of the wing velocity relative
to the air. Care has to be taken as to the sign of $\alpha$,
as evidenced by the two panels in \autoref{fig:kinematics2}.
When $\vec{v}_w \cdot \vec{s} > \vec{c} \cdot \vec{s}$, $\alpha$ has the correct sign
when defined from $\vec{v}_w$ to $\vec{c}$. When $\vec{v}_w \cdot \vec{s} < \vec{c} \cdot \vec{s}$,
it has the correct sign when defined from $\vec{c}$ to $\vec{v}_w$.
The following expression gives the correct sign:
\begin{align}
    \alpha = \sin^{-1} \left[\left( \frac{\vec{v}_w}{v_w} \times \vec{c} \right) \cdot \left( \vec{s} \times \vec{n} \right)\right]
\end{align}
Lift and drag coefficients are then obtained by
\begin{align}
    C_L(\alpha) &= C_{L,0} \sin 2\alpha \label{eq:cl} \\
    C_D(\alpha) &= C_{D,0} \cos^2 \alpha + C_{D,\frac{\pi}{2}} \sin^2 \alpha \label{eq:cd}
\end{align}
with $C_{L,0} = 1.5$, $C_{D,0} = 0.1$, $C_{D,\frac{\pi}{2}} = 2$,
as suggested by numerical simulations and experiments
across the full range of angle of attack~\cite{wang2004, wang2008}.
The lift and drag vectors are, respectively,
\begin{align}
    \vec{d} &= -\frac{\vec{v}_w}{v_w} \\
    \vec{\ell} &= \vec{d} \times \left( \vec{s} \times \vec{n} \right)
\end{align}
And the aerodynamic force of a single wing is
\begin{align}
    \vec{F}_{a,w} &= \frac12 \rho_\text{air} A_w v_w^2 \left( C_D \vec{d} + C_L \vec{\ell} \right)
\end{align}


\subsection{Nondimensionalization}

The flying insect is subjected to gravity and the aerodynamic
force. The resulting equation of motion is
\begin{align}
    m \ddot{x} &= \frac12 \rho_\text{air} A_w \sum_i^{N_w} \left[ v_w^2 \left( C_D \vec{d} + C_L \vec{\ell} \right) \cdot \vec{x} \right]^{(i)} \\
    m \ddot{z} &= \frac12 \rho_\text{air} A_w \sum_i^{N_w} \left[ v_w^2 \left( C_D \vec{d} + C_L \vec{\ell} \right) \cdot \vec{z} \right]^{(i)} - m g
\end{align}
We nondimensionalize it using the body length $L$,
the body mass $m$, and the gravitational time scale $\sqrt{L/g}$.
The resulting dimensionless parameters, along with the already
dimensionless wing angles, are summarized in \autoref{tab:nondim_params}.
Ranges for the parameters
$L_w^*$, $A^* / m^*$, and $\omega^*$ were obtained from
the morphological data in~\cite{wakeling1997} and the wingbeat
frequency data in~\cite{aracheloff2024} for \emph{Anisoptera}.

\subsubsection{Expanded Expression}

The above is sufficient to implement the aerodynamic
force model in code, but it is useful to explicitly
write out the fully expanded expression to get
some analytical insights. First, the velocity relative
to the wing relative to the air, projected onto
the $(n,s)$ plane, is
\begin{align}
    \vec{v}_w = \Bigl[ \frac23 L_w \dot{\phi} + \cos \phi (\cos \gamma \dot{x} + \sin \gamma \dot{z}) \Bigr] \vec{s} + \Bigl[ \cos\gamma \dot{z} - \sin\gamma \dot{x} \Bigr] \vec{n} \label{eq:vw}
\end{align}
To compute $C_L$ and $C_D$, we need both $\sin\alpha$ and
$\cos\alpha$, which are given by
\begin{align}
    \sin\alpha &= \frac{\Bigl[ \frac23 L_w \dot{\phi} + \cos \phi (\cos \gamma \dot{x} + \sin \gamma \dot{z}) \Bigr] \cos\psi + \Bigl[ \cos\gamma \dot{z} - \sin\gamma \dot{x} \Bigr] \sin\psi}{\sqrt{\Bigl[ \frac23 L_w \dot{\phi} + \cos \phi (\cos \gamma \dot{x} + \sin \gamma \dot{z}) \Bigr]^2 + \Bigl[ \cos\gamma \dot{z} - \sin\gamma \dot{x} \Bigr]^2}
}\\
\cos\alpha &= \frac{-\Bigl[ \frac23 L_w \dot{\phi} + \cos \phi (\cos \gamma \dot{x} + \sin \gamma \dot{z}) \Bigr] \sin\psi + \Bigl[ \cos\gamma \dot{z} - \sin\gamma \dot{x} \Bigr] \cos\psi}{\sqrt{\Bigl[ \frac23 L_w \dot{\phi} + \cos \phi (\cos \gamma \dot{x} + \sin \gamma \dot{z}) \Bigr]^2 + \Bigl[ \cos\gamma \dot{z} - \sin\gamma \dot{x} \Bigr]^2}
}
\end{align}
Giving
\begin{align}
    v_w^2 C_L &= 2 v_w^2 C_{L,0} \sin\alpha \cos\alpha \\
              &= 2 C_{L,0} \left( \Bigl[ \cos\gamma \dot{z} - \sin\gamma \dot{x} \Bigr]^2 - \Bigl[ \frac23 L_w \dot{\phi} + \cos \phi (\cos \gamma \dot{x} + \sin \gamma \dot{z}) \Bigr]^2\right) \sin\psi \cos\psi\nonumber \\
              &\qquad + 2 C_{L,0} \Bigl[ \cos\gamma \dot{z} - \sin\gamma \dot{x} \Bigr]\Bigl[ \frac23 L_w \dot{\phi} + \cos \phi (\cos \gamma \dot{x} + \sin \gamma \dot{z}) \Bigr] (1 - 2 \sin^2 \psi)
\end{align}
\begin{align}
v_w^2 C_D &= v_w^2 \left[ C_{D,0} \cos^2\alpha + C_{D,\frac{\pi}{2}} \sin^2\alpha \right]
\end{align}

It is more revealing, though, to resolve the force directly onto the
stroke basis. Projecting $\vec{F}_{a,w}$ onto $\vec{s}$ and $\vec{n}$,
and using that $\vec{\ell}$ is $\vec{d}$ rotated by $-\frac\pi2$ within
the plane, the unit-vector normalizations cancel one power of $v_w$ and
leave the compact scalar form
\begin{align}
    F_s &= -\tfrac12 \rho_\text{air} A_w\, v_w \left( C_D v_s + C_L v_n \right) \label{eq:Fs}\\
    F_n &= \phantom{-}\tfrac12 \rho_\text{air} A_w\, v_w \left( C_L v_s - C_D v_n \right) \label{eq:Fn}
\end{align}
where $v_s$ and $v_n$ are the bracketed coefficients of $\vec{s}$ and
$\vec{n}$ in \autoref{eq:vw}, and $v_w = \sqrt{v_s^2 + v_n^2}$.

At zero body velocity the normal velocity vanishes, $v_n = 0$, and the
sweep carries the entire relative wind, $v_s = \tfrac23 L_w \dot\phi$,
$v_w = |v_s|$. The coefficients then lose their dependence on the stroke
direction,
\begin{align}
    C_L = -C_{L,0} \sin 2\psi, \qquad
    C_D = C_{D,0}\sin^2\psi + C_{D,\frac\pi2}\cos^2\psi,
\end{align}
and the force collapses to
\begin{align}
    F_s = -\tfrac12 \rho_\text{air} A_w\, C_D\, v_s |v_s|, \qquad
    F_n = \phantom{-}\tfrac12 \rho_\text{air} A_w\, C_L\, v_s |v_s|. \label{eq:Fsn0}
\end{align}
The signed square $v_s|v_s|$ is odd in $\dot\phi$: a nonzero
cycle-averaged force therefore survives only to the extent that the
pitch schedule $\psi(t)$ injects matching odd content---which is the
role of the phase $\delta_0$---while the direction-independent part of
$C_D$ contributes only an oscillating in-plane drag that averages to
zero.

For small $\phi$, $\vec{F}_{a,w}$ is effectively confined to
the $(x,z)$ plane. This renders the model equivalent to
a common approach in which the wing translates through its
beating motion instead of doing an angular sweep, like in~\cite{wetherbee2026}.
As $\phi$ increases, and for a given $\vec{v}_w$, the useful force
in the $(x,z)$ plane, i.e. $\vec{F}_{a,w} - (\vec{F}_{a,w} \cdot \vec{y}) \vec{y}$,
decreases in magnitude, as $\vec{s}$ becomes more aligned
with $\vec{y}$. In addition, the projected wing
velocity $\vec{v}_w$ itself also decreases if the body velocity
has a nonzero component along $\vec{s}$.
In any case, the combined force of all wings is always in
$(x,z)$ because of the symmetric wingbeat and absence of crosswind.

We can carry the cycle average through analytically in this same
small-$\phi$ limit. There the stroke basis stops rotating over a cycle,
$\vec{s} \to \vec{e}_\parallel = \cos\gamma\,\vec{x} + \sin\gamma\,\vec{z}$
and $\vec{n} \to \vec{e}_\perp = \cos\gamma\,\vec{z} - \sin\gamma\,\vec{x}$,
so the cycle average of the force vector is simply the average of each
scalar component along a fixed direction. Writing $\tau = \omega t$, the
element speed is $v_s = u\cos\tau$ with amplitude $u = s_0 \omega$ and
sweep amplitude $s_0 = \tfrac23 L_w \phi_1$, so that
$v_s|v_s| = u^2 g(\tau)$ with the signed-square profile
$g(\tau) = \cos\tau\,|\cos\tau|$. With
$\psi(\tau) = \psi_0 + \psi_1 \sin(\tau - \delta_0)$ and
$\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle = \tfrac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}(\cdot)\,d\tau$, the
mean components from \autoref{eq:Fsn0} are
\begin{align}
    \langle F_n\rangle &= -\tfrac12 \rho_\text{air} A_w u^2\, C_{L,0}\,
        \langle \sin 2\psi\; g \rangle, \\
    \langle F_s\rangle &= -\tfrac12 \rho_\text{air} A_w u^2\,
        \frac{C_{D,\frac\pi2} - C_{D,0}}{2}\, \langle \cos 2\psi\; g \rangle,
\end{align}
where the constant part of $C_D$ has dropped out because
$\langle g\rangle = 0$.

Summing the identical contributions of the wings and collecting the
morphological and frequency factors into the dimensionless dynamic
pressure $q^* = \tfrac14 \frac{A^*}{m^*}\left(s_0^* \omega^*\right)^2$ of
\autoref{eq:qstar} (with dimensionless sweep amplitude
$s_0^* = \tfrac23 L_w^* \phi_1$), the mean force magnitude separates
exactly into a dynamic pressure and a pitch factor, $F_a^* = q^* C_\psi$,
with
\begin{align}
    C_\psi(\psi_0, \psi_1, \delta_0) = 2\sqrt{
        C_{L,0}^2\, \langle \sin 2\psi\; g\rangle^2
        + \left(\frac{C_{D,\frac\pi2} - C_{D,0}}{2}\right)^2
          \langle \cos 2\psi\; g\rangle^2 }. \label{eq:cpsi_analytic}
\end{align}
This is the central simplification: the pitch coefficient $C_\psi$ is a
function of the three pitch parameters alone, with the wing loading,
length, and beat frequency entirely absorbed into $q^*$. The small
residual scatter seen in \autoref{fig:separation} is exactly the
$O(\phi^2)$ rotation of the stroke basis that this limit discards.

Two limits confirm the expression. For a square-wave pitch,
$\sin 2\psi \to \operatorname{sign}(\cos\tau)$ locked to the stroke and
the drag term vanishes, giving
$\langle \operatorname{sign}(\cos\tau)\, g\rangle = \tfrac12$ and
$C_\psi \to C_{L,0}$. For the symmetric sinusoidal pitch $\psi_0 = 0$,
$\delta_0 = \tfrac\pi2$, the $\cos 2\psi$ term vanishes by parity and the
coefficient reduces to the single integral
\begin{align}
    C_\psi = \frac{4 C_{L,0}}{\pi} \int_0^{\pi/2}
        \sin\!\left(2\psi_1 \cos\tau\right) \cos^2\tau \, d\tau,
\end{align}
maximized at $\psi_1 \approx 51^\circ$ with $C_\psi \approx 1.43$, in
agreement with the numerical optimum of \autoref{fig:cpsi}.

\section{Two Analytical Cases}

\subsection{Stationary Flight}

In stationary flight, or \emph{hovering}, the wingbeat-averaged
aerodynamic force vector is opposed to and equal in magnitude to
the weight vector. There are residual oscillations of the body
due to fluctuations in the aerodynamic force over a wingbeat cycle,
and inertial coupling between the body and wings (not modeled here).
Assuming the corresponding body velocities are small compared to the
characteristic wing velocity $\frac23 L_w^* \phi_1 \omega^*$, we can approximate
the mean aerodynamic force analytically by setting $\dot{x}^* = \dot{z}^* = 0$.
As a result, the angle of attack is a simple function of the pitch
angle $\psi$ and the instantaneous sign of the flapping velocity:
\begin{align}
    \alpha = - \frac{\dot{\phi}}{|\dot{\phi}|} \left[ \frac{\pi}{2} - \psi \right]
\end{align}
After making use of the appropriate trigonometric identities, this
gives the lift and drag coefficients
\begin{align}
    C_L &= \frac{\dot{\phi}}{|\dot{\phi}|} C_{L,0} \sin 2\psi \\
    C_D &= C_{D,0} \sin^2 \psi + C_{D,\frac{\pi}{2}} \cos^2 \psi \nonumber \\
        &= \frac{C_{D,\frac{\pi}{2}} + C_{D,0}}{2} + \frac{C_{D,\frac{\pi}{2}} - C_{D,0}}{2} \cos 2\psi
\end{align}
The aerodynamic force normal to the stroke plane (along $\vec{n}$)
is pure drag, and the force within the stroke plane (along $\vec{s}$)
is pure lift. Denoting these two components as $F_n^*$ and $F_s^*$,
respectively, they are
\begin{align}
    F_n^* &= \frac12 \frac{A_w^*}{m^*} \left( \frac23 L_w^* \dot{\phi} \right)^2 C_L \\
    F_s^* &= - \frac{\dot{\phi}}{|\dot{\phi}|} \frac12 \frac{A_w^*}{m^*} \left( \frac23 L_w^* \dot{\phi} \right)^2 C_D
\end{align}
At this point, it is useful to linearize, in a sense, about the $\phi = 0$
position, by treating $\vec{s}$ as a fixed vector, parallel to the $(x,z)$
plane. This is a common modeling choice, see \emph{e.g.} \cite{wetherbee2026}.
The effect of the simplification is that force is increasingly
overestimated as the wingbeat amplitude $\phi_1$ increases. The effect is
small for reasonable values of $\phi_1$.
Note the numerical model retains the moving $\vec{s}$ (wing beats along an arc,
not a straight line) and we only perform the simplification for the analytical
derivation, so that the time integral depends on $\phi_1$ only as a
simple scaling factor. Note further that the simplification
has no effect on the mean if the wing pitch is symmetric
for both halves of the stroke, since $\langle F_s^* \rangle$ would be zero regardless.

With the above simplification made, we introduce the
the sweep kinematics $\phi(t^*) = \phi_1 \sin \omega^* t^*$,
giving $\dot{\phi} = \phi_1 \omega^* \cos \omega^* t^*$.
With the notation $\tau^* = \omega^* t^*$, we evaluate the
wingbeat cycle integral $\langle\cdot\rangle = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^{2\pi} (\cdot) d\tau^*$.
An important question is how to deal with the sign flips
due to the $\dot{\phi}/\phi$ factors appearing in both $F_n^*$
(through $C_L$) and $F_s^*$. We can actually fold them
into the "velocity squared" factors as follows
\begin{align}
    \frac{\dot{\phi}}{|\dot\phi|} \dot{\phi}^2 &= \dot{\phi} |\dot{\phi}| \nonumber \\
                                              &= \phi_1 \omega^* \cos\tau^* |\cos\tau^*|
\end{align}
It is also convenient to introduce the wingbeat amplitude
shorthand $s_0^* = \frac23 L_w^* \phi_1$. Evaluating the integrals, we obtain
\begin{align}
    \langle F_n^* \rangle &= \frac12 \frac{A_w^*}{m^*} \left( s_0^* \omega^* \right)^2 C_{L,0} \langle \sin 2\psi\; \cos \tau^* |\cos \tau^*| \rangle, \\
    \langle F_s^* \rangle &= \frac12 \frac{A_w^*}{m^*} \left( s_0^* \omega^* \right)^2 \frac{C_{D,\frac\pi2} - C_{D,0}}{2}\, \langle \cos 2\psi\; \cos^2 \tau^* \rangle,
\end{align}

\subsection{Stroke-Plane-Normal Flight}

\section{Stationary Flight}

Main results:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Effect of pitch parameters $(\psi_0, \psi_1, \delta_0)$ on
        mean aerodynamic force is separable from morphological parameters
        and wingbeat frequency
    \item Pitch parameters $\psi_0 = 0^\circ$, $\psi_1 \approx 51^\circ$,
        and $\delta_0 = 90^\circ$ maximize mean aerodynamic force
        given $(\frac{A^*}{m^*}, L_w^*, \omega^*)$
    \item A two-variable proportional scheme using $\phi_1$ for force magnitude
        and $\psi_0$ for force direction is sufficient to achieve stable hovering
    \item Inclined stroke plane hover exhibits increased damping in
        the vertical direction compared to a horizontal stroke plane
    \item Power efficiency, measured by a cost of endurance $P^*$, is a
        distinct objective from force production ($C_\psi$): the force-maximizing
        pitch is not the power-minimizing one, and inclined-stroke hover costs
        \emph{more} power in this quasi-steady model, even though drag takes on
        an increasing share of the weight as in \cite{wang2004}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Equilibrium Solutions}

A good first step for mapping the parameter space
might be to identify those parameter values that
give an average aerodynamic force over a cycle that is equal
to or greater than the weight, i.e. $F_a^* = \frac{F_a}{m_b g} \geq 1$.
Outside of this group of solutions,
hovering and meaningful maneuvering flight cannot be achieved.
This is independent of the
stroke plane angle $\gamma_0$ and the fore/hind phase
shift $\sigma_0$, which limits us to just 7 parameters.
We can further eliminate one parameter by collapsing
$L_w^*$ and $\phi_1$ into a single wing displacement
amplitude parameter $s_0^* = \frac23 L_w^* \phi_1$
(displacement at $\frac23$-span station, aerodynamic center)
resulting in 6 morphological and wingbeat parameters.

\subsubsection*{Verification Against Analytical Solution}

As a first, quick verification we compare the
$F_a^* = 1$ contour result in the ($A^*/m^*$, $\omega^*$) plane
to the simplified analytical result of \cite{wetherbee2026}
for a translating element with constant $45^\circ$ pitch through each
beat half:
\begin{align}
    \left(F_a^*\right)_\text{constant pitch} = \frac{C_{L,0}}{4} \frac{A^*}{m^*} \left(s_0^* \omega^*\right)^2
\end{align}
Note $A^* = 4 A_w^*$. We compare our model with the above expression
at the reference condition from \cite{wetherbee2026}:
$s_0^* = 1.5$ (achieved in practice with a small $\phi_1$ and a large $L_w^*$),
$\psi_0 = 0^\circ$, $\psi_1 = 45^\circ$, and $\delta_0 = 90^\circ$.
Crucially we do this assuming zero body velocity: the velocity
of the wings relative to the air is equal to the velocity
of the wings relative to the body.
Contours are shown in \autoref{fig:contour_check}.
Using a single wing element at the $\frac23$-span station,
there is very close agreement, as expected, with a small difference
attributable to the sinusoidal wing pitch (as opposed to a square wave).
The wing element spends less time at large angle of attack
so the mean force is slightly lower.
As the number of elements is increased $F_a^*$ converges to a
lower value (by about 25\%), causing the contour to shift towards
higher $A^*/m^*$ and $\omega^*$. We use a single element
at $\frac23-$span throughout for simplicity.

\subsubsection*{Separating the Effects of the Parameters on $F_a^*$}

The present model allows for more complex wing motions
than a simple symmetric stroke: the mean pitching
angle $\psi_0$ may be nonzero, and the phase shift $\delta_0$
between the pitching and flapping motions may be
different from 90$^\circ$. One question we can ask is whether
the contribution of the pitch to the force magnitude $F_a^*$
can be represented as a coefficient that would depend on $(\psi_0, \psi_1, \delta_0)$,
independent of the other 3 parameters. Looking back
to the simple analytical expression for $F_a^*$ for constant pitch,
we can extract the dimensionless dynamic pressure
\begin{align}
    q^* = \frac14 \frac{A^*}{m^*} \left(s_0^* \omega^*\right)^2 \label{eq:qstar}
\end{align}
We can then evaluate $F_a^* / q^*$ and check whether it is independent
of ($\frac{A^*}{m^*}$, $s_0^*$, $\omega^*$) over the parameter space,
i.e.\ whether the two groups of variables can be cleanly separated.
Again, we do this first at zero body velocity, such that $F_a^* = 1$,
applied in the right direction, would result in a hover.
As can be seen in \autoref{fig:separation}, the separation is clean, with
small amounts of scatter associated with the nonlinearity of
the flapping motion. The right side of the plot is obtained
by picking a reference amplitude ($s_0^* = 0.25$, or $\phi_1 \approx 30^\circ$)
to evaluate $F_a^*$. The sensitivity
to the choice of $s_0^*$ is very small, about 1 to 2\%.
The result is completely independent of $A^* / m^*$ and $\omega^*$
because they appear purely as multiplicative factors in the analytical expression.
It might be clearer to do a purely analytical treatment of this
part.

\subsubsection*{Optimal Wing Pitch Parameters}

This decomposition is also a first handle on the power efficiency
of the wingbeat. A given net force can be achieved with a faster
or slower wingbeat depending on the wing pitch pattern, and a higher
$C_\psi$ lets the wing beat more slowly for the same force. As we
make precise later (\autoref{sec:power}), $C_\psi$ is not by itself
the efficiency---the cost also depends on how much drag the pattern
incurs---but it enters that cost strongly (as $C_\psi^{-3/2}$), so
higher-$C_\psi$ patterns are energetically favored, all else being equal.
Looking again at the simplified case from earlier it seems
the optimum should sit near $\psi_0 = 0$, $\psi_1 = 45^\circ$, $\delta_0 = 90^\circ$,
since a $45^\circ$ angle of attack maximizes lift (limit case
for square wave pitch: $C_\psi = C_{L,0}$).

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{../figures/pitch_efficiency.light.png}
    \caption{Dependence of $C_\psi$ on pitch parameters at zero body velocity. Optimal value shown as white dots.}\label{fig:cpsi}
\end{figure}

We show the dependence of
$C_\psi$ on the three parameters in \autoref{fig:cpsi}. Again, $C_\psi$
here is obtained by taking $s_0^* = 0.25$, but this value has little
effect on $C_\psi$ as seen in the scatter plot from earlier.
The result is what we expect, with $\psi_1$ slightly larger than
$45^\circ$ to provide a mean angle of attack that is closer
to $45^\circ$. The dependence of $F_a^*$ on $\delta_0$ is symmetric
but the force direction depends on the sign, we highlight $\delta_0 = +90^\circ$
since with our conventions it results in a force in the direction of the leading edge.
This optimal value of $C_\psi$ is slightly smaller than $C_{L,0}$.
Note that because the optimal pitch pattern is symmetric, the drag coefficient
has no effect (drag component cancels out over the two halves of the wingbeat).
We notice that with more wing elements, the optimal $C_\psi$ is lower,
converging to a value just above unity. 
This suggests the following approximate criterion for hover feasibility,
which may also extend to maneuverability
\begin{align}
    \frac14 \frac{A^*}{m^*} \left(s_0^* \omega^*\right)^2 \gtrsim 1
\end{align}
The corresponding 3D surface is shown in \autoref{fig:criterion}.
The required wingbeat amplitude $\phi_1$ for a given $(\frac{A^*}{m^*}, \omega^*)$
can be recovered by
\begin{align*}
    \phi_1 = \frac32 \frac{s_0^*}{L_w^*}
\end{align*}
The very high amplitudes ($\phi_1 \approx 60^\circ$) associated with low values
of both $\frac{A^*}{m^*}$ and $\omega^*$ suggest such combinations
probably do not occur, and that the parameter space can be further restricted.
The data source for $\omega^*$ does not report measurements necessary
to obtain $\frac{A^*}{m^*}$, but we could maybe find other sources.
We can estimate the typical hover wingbeat amplitude
by assuming that the lowest $\omega^*$ occur at the highest
$\frac{A^*}{m^*}$. For this set of parameters ($\omega^* = 8$ and
$\frac{A^*}{m^*} = 1.0$) the amplitude is $s_0^* \approx 0.23$,
which corresponds to $\phi_1 \approx 26^\circ$.

\subsubsection*{Nonzero Mean Wing Pitch for Inclined Stroke Plane Hover}

$C_\psi$ may deviate from its ``optimum'' value. At this
value, the wingbeat is symmetric, meaning the net force
vector is normal to the stroke. This means hovering at the
optimal $C_\psi$ requires a horizontal stroke plane. 
Dragonflies however are known to hover with an inclined
stroke plane, which could be due to efficiency considerations
that we have ignored (picking the maximum $C_\psi$ may not
be exactly optimal energy-wise, even if it minimizes $s_0$,
or our model simply does not capture other real effects
that lead to the inclined stroke plane being advantageous).
Without going too deep into these energy considerations
at least for now, it is interesting at this point to
consider the effect of the pitch parameters not just on the
force magnitude but on its \emph{direction}
relative to the stroke plane. With an inclined stroke
(angle $\gamma_0$ to the horizontal), hover (or vertical
flight) requires the net aerodynamic force vector to be
angled by $+\gamma_0$ to the stroke plane normal.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{../figures/force_direction_test.light.png}
    \caption{Effect of $\psi_0$ on force direction and wing stroke efficiency.}\label{fig:beta_vs_psi0}
\end{figure}

We denote the force direction angle relative to the stroke
plane with $\beta$. We find numerically (and could probably show analytically)
that $\beta$ depends only on the mean pitch angle
$\psi_0$, and on the sign of $\delta_0$. To be exact,
there is a slight variation in $\beta(\psi_0)$ over the range
of $\psi_1$ and $|\delta_0|$ on the order of $0.1^\circ$
to $1^\circ$ depending on the wingbeat amplitude.
We also find that the $(\psi_1, \delta_0)$ that maximizes
$C_\psi$ is invariant with $\psi_0$.
We show $\beta(\psi_0)$ in \autoref{fig:beta_vs_psi0}
along with the optimal $C_\psi$ at a given $\psi_0$.

Three observations from the plot: (a) $\beta$ is monotonic in
$\psi_0$, so $\psi_0$ can be used conveniently as a control
variable for the force direction, in addition to the stroke plane
angle $\gamma_0$, (b) a $90^\circ$ turn in $\psi_0$ reverses the
force direction, so only moderate changes in $\psi_0$ are needed
to achieve relatively large changes in $\beta$ (there is factor of $\sim2$),
and (c) the wing stroke is most efficient when
the mean pitch attitude points out-of-plane or in-plane, and least efficient when
it forms a $45^\circ$ angle with the stroke plane.

\subsubsection*{Power Efficiency}%
\label{sec:power}

$C_\psi$ measures how much cycle-averaged force the wingbeat
produces per unit dynamic pressure; it is an effective lift
coefficient, not an energetic efficiency. The preceding result
that inclined-stroke hover costs $C_\psi$ raises the natural
question of whether it also costs power. To address this we add
an energetic measure, following \cite{wang2008}.

With massless wings the only mechanical cost is the work done
against aerodynamic drag: lift is perpendicular to the air-relative
velocity and does no work, and the pitch-axis torque is dropped along
with the wing inertia (the inertial cost vanishes over a cycle for the
near-symmetric pitch reversal, as noted by \cite{wang2008}). For one
element the instantaneous aerodynamic power is
\begin{align}
    P = |\vec{F}_d|\, v_w = \tfrac12 \rho_\text{air} A_w v_w^3 C_D(\alpha),
\end{align}
with $v_w$ the same in-plane air-relative speed that enters the force
law. Summing over wings and averaging over a cycle gives the mean
aerodynamic power $\bar{P}^*$ (in units of $m_b g \sqrt{gL}$). Following
\cite{wang2008} we form the dimensionless \emph{cost of endurance}, the
power required to support unit weight at the reference speed
$U_\text{ref} = \sqrt{2 m_b g / \rho_\text{air} A}$,
\begin{align}
    P^* = \frac{\bar{P}^*}{U_\text{ref}^*}, \qquad
    U_\text{ref}^* = \sqrt{\frac{2}{A^*/m^*}}.
\end{align}
For a steadily translating wing supporting its weight by lift this
reduces to the familiar $P^* = C_D / C_L^{3/2}$, and it is the quantity
to minimize. Imposing the hover constraint $F_a^* = q^* C_\psi = 1$
eliminates the amplitude scale ($A^*/m^*$, $s_0^*$, $\omega^*$ enter
only through $q^*$), leaving a pure function of the kinematics
\begin{align}
    P^* = 2\sqrt{2}\;
    \frac{\left\langle |\cos \omega t|^3\, C_D(\alpha) \right\rangle}{C_\psi^{3/2}},
    \label{eq:cost}
\end{align}
the flapping analogue of $C_D / C_L^{3/2}$: the velocity-weighted drag
coefficient in the numerator plays the role of $C_D$, and the force
coefficient $C_\psi$ plays the role of $C_L$.

Two consequences follow. First, $C_\psi$ is related to but not identical
with efficiency. Through \autoref{eq:cost} the cost falls as
$C_\psi^{-3/2}$, so a larger force coefficient helps, but the
drag-power factor matters too and the two optima differ. Holding the
force vertical, $P^*$ keeps decreasing as the pitch amplitude $\psi_1$
is raised past the $C_\psi$ peak ($\psi_1 \approx 51^\circ$): the
power optimum sits at the upper edge of the morphological range
($\psi_1 \approx 60^\circ$, and $\approx 77^\circ$ if unconstrained),
where the wingbeat is about 20\% cheaper than at the $C_\psi$-optimal
pitch. The extra angle of attack is added near stroke reversal, where
the wing is slow and the $v^3$ weighting in \autoref{eq:cost} is small,
so it sheds drag power faster than it sheds force. This is the
quasi-steady counterpart of the observation of \cite{wang2008} that
maximizing the averaged lift-to-drag ratio is not the same as
minimizing the aerodynamic power.

Second---and this answers the question above---inclined-stroke hover
\emph{does} cost power in this model. \autoref{fig:power}(a) shows $P^*$
against $\gamma_0$ for both the force-optimal pitch (the $C_\psi^\text{max}$
strategy used elsewhere in this report) and the power-optimal pitch;
in both cases $P^*$ rises with $\gamma_0$, by roughly 50 to 70\% from
$\gamma_0 = 0$ to $40^\circ$. The drag mechanism of \cite{wang2004} is
nonetheless present: as the stroke tilts, the two half-strokes' drag no
longer cancels vertically and drag takes on a growing share of the
weight, reaching 75\% at $\gamma_0 = 60^\circ$
(\autoref{fig:power}(b))---in close quantitative agreement with the 76\%
at $63^\circ$ reported by \cite{wang2004}. But the drag recruited this
way is expensive force, generated at high angle of attack where $C_D$ is
large, so the net force coefficient $C_\psi$ falls and, through the
$C_\psi^{-3/2}$ dependence, the cost rises.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{../figures/power_efficiency.light.png}
    \caption{Hover power efficiency vs. stroke-plane angle, reference
    configuration. (a) Cost of endurance $P^*$ for the force-optimal
    (maximum-$C_\psi$) pitch and for the power-optimal pitch with a
    sinusoidal and a square-wave feather, all re-trimmed to hover at each
    $\gamma_0$; the steady-flight ideal $C_D/C_L^{3/2}$ is shown for
    reference. (b) Share of the cycle-averaged vertical force carried by
    drag rather than lift, with the result of \cite{wang2004} marked.}%
    \label{fig:power}
\end{figure}

One might ask whether this is an artifact of the sinusoidal pitch, since
the cheap return stroke of an efficient hoverer is a \emph{feathered}
stroke---one held at a near-constant, small angle of attack---which a
sinusoid coupled to the sweep cannot produce. Flattening the feather
toward a square wave (\autoref{fig:power}(a), dash-dotted), which holds a
constant pitch on each half-stroke, helps but does not change the
conclusion. It is cheaper at every $\gamma_0$, because each half-stroke
can sit near its best angle of attack instead of sweeping through the
wasteful high-$\alpha$ region at mid-stroke; at $\gamma_0 = 0$ it costs
$P^* \approx 0.47$ against the sinusoid's $0.59$. And it \emph{does}
feather the return: the power-optimal square wave at $\gamma_0 = 40^\circ$
holds one half-stroke at a mean angle of attack of $61^\circ$, which
carries 92\% of the cycle's power, and the other at just $7^\circ$,
carrying only 8\%---a nearly free return stroke that the sinusoid cannot
make (its lighter half stays near $23^\circ$). Yet $P^*$ still climbs
with $\gamma_0$, from $0.47$ to $0.91$ over $0$ to $40^\circ$. The
obstacle is the other half-stroke: aiming the resultant vertically forces
the \emph{powered} stroke to over-pitch to $\approx 60^\circ$, well past
the angle of best lift-to-drag, and that penalty outweighs the saving
from the free return. A horizontal stroke sidesteps it entirely---the two
half-strokes' drag cancels, so both can run near the optimal angle---which
is why it stays the cheapest hover here.

This still falls short of \cite{wang2004}, who finds the specific power
of hovering roughly independent of stroke-plane angle up to $\approx
60^\circ$. The gap is principally unsteady aerodynamics: that flat
specific power comes from full Navier--Stokes computations, and
\cite{wang2004} notes the favorable behavior up to $\beta \approx
60^\circ$ is not captured by a quasi-steady model---which is what we have
used throughout. The cheapest hover here is about $1.4$ times the
steady-optimal $C_D/C_L^{3/2}$, and within this quasi-steady model the
inclined stroke plane---even with a feathered return---is not explained
by power efficiency. It remains, as far as the present analysis goes, a
means of force-direction control.

\subsubsection*{Hover Control Variables}

The above insights suggest a few possible ways to achieve
hover control. Candidates for control variables can be split into
two groups: those that control force magnitude ($\phi_1, \omega^*$ through
$q^*$ and $\psi_1, \delta_0$ through $C_\psi$), and those that control force
direction ($\gamma_0$, $\psi_0$). Note this is not such a clean
split, because $\psi_0$ also changes force magnitude through $C_\psi$,
just more weakly than it changes direction. For simplicity, we
would rather consider $\omega^*$ to be a fixed parameter, so we will exclude it
at least for now.
Since we also do not fully understand the factors
that set the hovering stroke plane angle, we will also
consider $\gamma_0$ to be fixed for now (later on, we
compare the effectiveness of $\gamma_0$ and $\psi_0$
as directional control variables).
In addition, it is beneficial to keep $C_\psi$ at or near its maximum
value, which sets $\psi_1$ and $\delta_0$. This leaves $\phi_1$ (or $s_0^*$)
for magnitude and $\psi_0$ for direction. At equilibrium,
$\psi_0$ will be such that $\beta = +\gamma_0$ (force is vertical),
and $\phi_1$ such that $F_a^* = 1$. \autoref{tab:hover_psi0} shows
the required value of $\psi_0$ for different $\gamma_0$,
for a reference configuration $L_w^* = 0.75$, $\frac{A^*}{m^*} = 0.6$,
and $\omega^* = 14$. Different values of these parameters impose
a different $\phi_1$ for hover, which results in small
changes in $\psi_0$ up to a few percent.

\subsection{Hover Dynamics and Control}

\subsubsection*{Unsteady Dynamics}

Before designing a controller, we look at how the actual, 
unsteady hover dynamics differ from the equilibrium picture
we have been working with so far. Indeed the body velocity
will not be exactly zero throughout, and will oscillate
according to the fluctuations of the aerodynamic force
over a wingbeat cycle. There is an additional effect due to
small but nonzero mass of the wings, which will make the
body bob up and down in the direction of the flapping motion.
We have neglected this in our work so far. In this part we will
evaluate the relative importance of the two effects on the
body oscillations.

A key parameter here is the fore/hind wing phase shift $\sigma_0$.
We expect the oscillations to be more pronounced
when the wings beat in-phase ($\sigma_0 = 0^\circ$) than
when they beat out-of-phase ($\sigma_0 = 180^\circ$).
In fact, if the forewing and hindwing motions are identical
except for the phase shift, the wing inertia effect will be zero
(true for translation, not for body pitch, which we do not consider
in the present model).

We introduce a relative wing mass parameter $m_w^* = m_w / m$.
According to \cite{wakeling1997}, values range from 0.01 to 0.03 for \emph{Anisoptera}.
We run one hover wingbeat to obtain the body oscillation peak-to-peak amplitude,
comparing the ideal massless wing case to the $m_w^* = 0.02$ case,
for the reference configuration of \autoref{tab:ref_config} (\autoref{fig:oscillations}).
The wing mass and aerodynamic contributions to the
body oscillations appear to be of the same order,
except, as expected, when the wings beat out-of-phase.
We will consider $m_w^* = 0$ in the following, but
should keep in mind that this is only effectively true
at $\sigma_0 = 180^\circ$.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{../figures/inertia_vs_aero_sigma0.light.png}
    \caption{Effect of $\sigma_0$ and $m_w^*$ on body oscillations during hover.}\label{fig:oscillations}
\end{figure}

Another effect of the aerodynamic force oscillations is positional drift.
For a constant, open-loop wingbeat pattern, the dragonfly settles
in a steady-state that has a small but nonzero mean body velocity,
when the equilibrium prediction is that the zero mean force condition
occurs at exactly zero mean body velocity.

Overall these effects are relatively mild, with instantaneous body velocities
of approximately $0.25 s_0^* \omega^*$ in the worst case scenario ($\sigma_0 = 0^\circ$,
$\omega^* = 8$, $A^* / m^* = 1.0$), and only about $\frac{1}{10}$ of that
for $\sigma_0 = 180^\circ$, so the conclusions drawn from the equilibrium
analysis should be applicable to controller design.

\subsubsection*{Hover Controller}

Recall we identified $\phi_1$ and $\psi_0$ as the two control
variables for hover. $\phi_1$ controls force magnitude, and
$\psi_0$ controls force direction, with the secondary effect
of reducing the force magnitude as it moves away from the stroke
plane normal mean $\psi_0 = 0$. It seems natural to use $\phi_1$
to control altitude, and $\psi_0$ to control fore-aft drift
by tilting the force direction vector from the vertical.
Specifically we propose the following control scheme
\begin{align*}
    \psi_0 &= \psi_0^\text{eq} - K_x (x - x_0) \\
    \phi_1 &= \phi_1^\text{eq} - K_z (z - z_0)
\end{align*}
Note that orienting the force in the $-x$ direction requires
pitching up (increasing $\psi_0$), so we will need $K_x < 0$.
To design the proportional gains for the reference configuration
(\autoref{tab:ref_config}) we begin by forming the Jacobian matrix
of the wingbeat-averaged aerodynamic force $\vec{F}_a^*$
at the hover equilibrium, using finite differences,
to evaluate the control effectiveness of the two variables
about the force axes. The Jacobian is
\begin{align*}
    J = \begin{bmatrix}
        \displaystyle \frac{\partial F^*_{a,x}}{\partial \psi_0} &\displaystyle  \frac{\partial F^*_{a,x}}{\partial \phi_1} \\
        \displaystyle  \frac{\partial F^*_{a,z}}{\partial \psi_0} &  \displaystyle \frac{\partial F^*_{a,z}}{\partial \phi_1}
        \end{bmatrix}
        \approx \begin{bmatrix}
            -2.06 & -0.05 \\
            +0.93 & 5.83
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{align*}
Notably $\phi_1$ has a negligible effect on force tilt
from the vertical ($F^*_{a,x}$), while as we pointed out before $\psi_0$
does have a significant secondary effect on $F^*_{a,z}$.
Since $\phi_1$ cleanly drives $F^*_{a,z}$, its control
loop will absorb the $\psi_0$ cross term.
The decoupled dynamics are (note mass matrix is $I$ because of
nondimensionalization)
\begin{align*}
    \ddot{x}^* &= -2.06 \times \delta \psi_0 \\
    \ddot{z}^* &= 5.83 \times \delta \phi_1
\end{align*}
So $\omega_n^2 = -2.06 K_x = 5.83 K_z$. We want $\omega_n$
slow enough that the controller sees the wingbeat-averaged
force and not the fluctuations. Using $\omega_n = 1.5$ (10
times smaller than the reference wingbeat frequency $\omega^* = 14$)
we get
\begin{align*}
    K_x &= -1.09 \\
    K_z &= 0.39
\end{align*}
The step response is shown in \autoref{fig:hover_step}. The figure
shows the reference configuration along with low and high wing
loading configuration. $A^*/m^*$ was chosen as the variable here because
it had the greatest effect on the damping of the response.
The response is very noticeably more damped as $A^* / m^*$ increases.
Over the range of parameters, using the same gains designed for the
reference configuration, the achieved $\omega_n$ was close to the
design value of 1.5, and the response did not vary very much
except along the $A^* / m^*$ axis. The controller was stable across
the full parameter space.
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{../figures/hover_control_step.light.png}
    \caption{Effect of $A^* / m^*$ on hover controller step response.}\label{fig:hover_step}
\end{figure}

A more interesting result is the influence of stroke plane angle
on damping, as shown in \autoref{fig:hover_step_gamma}. It appears
that damping is strongest within the stroke plane,
which means that vertical damping in the $\gamma_0 = 0^\circ$

Horizontal damping decreases accordingly, with the end
result being that there is a better match in the damping
characteristics along both axes.
Perhaps this is another advantage of hovering
with an inclined stroke plane.

\section{Moving and Maneuvering Flight}

So far we have focused on stationary flight, with at
most small amplitude motions about a stationary or
slowly drifting position. That is the case where the
body velocity relative to the air is much less than
the wing velocity relative to the body $(v_b^* \ll s_0^* \omega^*)$.
Since we are interested not just in hover but in maneuvering flight,
we need to consider the case where $v_b^* \sim s_0^* \omega^*$, perhaps even
$v_b^* \gg s_0^*$.

\subsection{Equilibrium Solutions}

\subsubsection*{Velocity Parameterization}

We characterize the body velocity in the stroke-plane frame,
by its angle $\chi$ from the stroke-plane normal and by the
advance ratio
\begin{align*}
    J = \frac{v_b^*}{s_0^* \omega^*}
\end{align*}
i.e.\ the body speed measured against the wing speed scale.
This choice has two motivations. First, the $\gamma_0$-invariance
of the force magnitude carries over: $|\vec{F}_a^*|$ is unchanged
when the stroke plane and the velocity vector are rotated together
(verified numerically to machine precision), so a single map
in $(J, \chi)$ covers all stroke plane angles. Second, scaling the
speed with $s_0^* \omega^*$ keeps every velocity in the problem
proportional to the wing speed, which preserves the $q^*$
magnitude scaling: the effect of the body velocity can only
enter through the coefficient, which becomes
$C_\psi(\psi_0, \psi_1, \delta_0;\, J, \chi)$.

This last point assumes the separation $F_a^* = q^* C_\psi$
itself survives at nonzero velocity, which we check
in \autoref{fig:velocity_separation} by evaluating the scatter
of $F_a^* / q^*$ over random draws of
$(\frac{A^*}{m^*}, \omega^*, \phi_1, L_w^*)$ at fixed pitch
and fixed $(J, \chi)$. The residual is exactly zero for
axial inflow ($\chi = 0^\circ$ or $180^\circ$) and grows with
the \emph{in-plane} velocity component, reaching about 7\%
at $J = 0.5$ and 11\% at $J = 1$ for $\chi = 90^\circ$.
The distinction is geometric: an axial inflow meets the
flapping wing at the same relative orientation at every point
of the stroke arc, so the cycle geometry still depends on
amplitude only through $s_0^* \omega^*$, while an in-plane wind
makes an angle with the wing velocity that varies along the arc,
re-introducing a weak dependence on $\phi_1$ itself.
The separation therefore remains useful well into the
maneuvering regime, with the caveat that it is approximate
for fast in-plane flight.

    \centering

    body velocity. (a) $C_\psi$ at nominal pitch as a function of advance
    ratio for several inflow directions $\chi$. (b) Separation residual,
    evaluated as the coefficient of variation of $F_a^* / q^*$ over random
    draws of the morphological and wingbeat parameters at fixed pitch.}\label{fig:velocity_separation}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection*{Axial Inflow}

The simplest slice of the velocity plane is axial inflow, $\chi = 0$,
where the body moves along the stroke-plane normal. Dragonflies are
observed to hold roughly this configuration, with the stroke-plane
normal and the flight direction nearly aligned, so it is also the most
relevant slice. It is the one case in which the zero-velocity analysis
of \autoref{eq:cpsi_analytic} carries over essentially unchanged,
because the body velocity has no component along the in-plane sweep
direction $\vec{e}_\parallel$. The sweep term in $v_s$ is then untouched
by the body motion,
\begin{align}
    v_s = \tfrac23 L_w \dot\phi, \qquad v_n = \text{const},
\end{align}
so the $\cos\phi$ coupling that produced the residual scatter at
$\chi \neq 0$ disappears; this is exactly why the separation residual
was found to be \emph{zero} for axial inflow in
\autoref{fig:velocity_separation}. The wing sees an oscillating sweep
superposed on a uniform normal inflow---the classical
heaving-plus-inflow kinematics of a blade element---and the angle of
attack splits additively into the pitch and an inflow angle,
\begin{align}
    \alpha(\tau) = \psi(\tau) + \theta(\tau), \qquad
    \theta(\tau) = \operatorname{atan2}(v_s, v_n), \label{eq:alpha_inflow}
\end{align}
with $\tau = \omega t$. As the inflow vanishes ($J \to 0$) the inflow
angle returns to $\theta \to \pm\tfrac\pi2$ and the hover result is
recovered.

Writing the sweep-speed amplitude as $u = s_0 \omega$ and the normal
inflow through the advance ratio $J = v_b^* / (s_0^* \omega^*)$, we have
$v_s = u\cos\tau$, $v_n = J u$ for a body advancing along the cycle-mean
force, and $v_w = u\sqrt{\cos^2\tau + J^2}$. Substituting
into \autoref{eq:Fs} and \autoref{eq:Fn} and cycle-averaging in the
small-$\phi$ limit---where, as in \autoref{eq:cpsi_analytic}, the stroke
basis is frozen and the average of the vector equals the vector of the
averages---gives the moving-flight coefficient
\begin{align}
    C_\psi(\psi_0, \psi_1, \delta_0;\, J)
        &= 2\sqrt{\langle \tilde F_s\rangle^2 + \langle \tilde F_n\rangle^2}, \label{eq:cpsi_axial}\\
    \tilde F_n &= \sqrt{\cos^2\tau + J^2}\,\bigl(C_{L,0}\sin 2\alpha\,\cos\tau - J\,C_D\bigr), \\
    \tilde F_s &= -\sqrt{\cos^2\tau + J^2}\,\bigl(C_D \cos\tau + J\,C_{L,0}\sin 2\alpha\bigr),
\end{align}
where $\tilde F_s, \tilde F_n$ are the stroke-frame components in units
of $\tfrac12 \rho_\text{air} A_w u^2$ and $\alpha$ is given by
\autoref{eq:alpha_inflow}. The entire velocity dependence has collapsed
onto the single parameter $J$: along the axial line the five-variable
coefficient $C_\psi(\psi_0,\psi_1,\delta_0; J,\chi)$ reduces to a
one-parameter family.

A parity argument sharpens the picture. For symmetric pitch
($\psi_0 = 0$, $\delta_0 = \tfrac\pi2$) the substitution
$\tau \to \pi - \tau$ sends $v_s \to -v_s$, $\psi \to -\psi$, and hence
$\alpha \to -\alpha$ at fixed $v_n$, so the $\tilde F_s$ integrand is odd
and $\langle \tilde F_s\rangle = 0$: the cycle-mean force lies
\emph{exactly} along the stroke-plane normal at all advance ratios
(verified numerically to $0.01^\circ$). The aligned wingbeat therefore
produces a force purely along the flight axis; redirecting it---for
instance to support weight away from vertical flight---requires breaking
the symmetry through the mean pitch $\psi_0$, the same lever used in
hover. The coefficient then reduces to the single integral
\begin{align}
    C_\psi(J) = 2\left| \Bigl\langle \sqrt{\cos^2\tau + J^2}\,
        \bigl( C_{L,0}\sin 2\alpha\,\cos\tau - J\,C_D \bigr) \Bigr\rangle \right|,
\end{align}
which returns to \autoref{eq:cpsi_analytic} as $J\to0$ and decreases
monotonically with $J$ as the growing inflow detunes the angle of attack
and bleeds off force---the wing losing authority as it advances into its
own induced direction. The values track the numerical coefficient of
\autoref{fig:velocity_cpsi} along $\chi = 0$: $C_\psi = 1.43$ at hover,
$1.21$ at $J = 0.2$, and $0.90$ at $J = 0.4$.

\subsubsection*{$C_\psi$ over the Velocity Plane}

With $(J, \chi)$ added to the three pitch parameters, $C_\psi$
is a function of five variables, which we cannot map directly.
Three structural observations (all numerical) reduce the
visualization problem to a manageable size:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Mirror symmetry: $C_\psi(J, \chi, \psi_0) = C_\psi(J, -\chi, -\psi_0)$
        to machine precision, so at $\psi_0 = 0$ the velocity plane
        is symmetric about the stroke-plane normal and only a
        half-plane needs to be computed.
    \item The optimal pitch phase stays pinned at $\delta_0 = 90^\circ$
        across the entire velocity plane.
    \item Consequently only $\psi_1$ retunes with velocity, and it does
        so smoothly.
\end{enumerate}
Since $(J, \chi)$ is just the body velocity vector expressed in
the stroke-plane frame, we can treat the velocity plane as a disk
(radius $J$, angle $\chi$) and show each scalar of interest as one
polar map (\autoref{fig:velocity_cpsi}): (a) $C_\psi$ at the
\emph{hover-optimal} pitch, i.e.\ how the hover wingbeat performs
when the body moves; (b) $C_\psi$ with $\psi_1$ re-optimized at
each velocity within its allowed range; and (c) the re-optimized
$\psi_1$ itself.
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{../figures/velocity_cpsi_maps.light.png}
    \caption{$C_\psi$ over the body-velocity plane (radius: advance ratio $J$,
    angle: $\chi$ from the stroke-plane normal), at $\psi_0 = 0$,
    $\delta_0 = 90^\circ$, for the reference configuration.
    (a) Hover-optimal pitch ($\psi_1 = 51^\circ$), dashed line marks $C_\psi = 1$.
    (b) $\psi_1$ re-optimized at each velocity over the range of \autoref{tab:nondim_params}.
    (c) The re-optimized $\psi_1$.}\label{fig:velocity_cpsi}
\end{figure}

The maps show three regimes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Axial inflow ($\chi$ near $0^\circ$)}: the inflow
        through the stroke plane washes out the angle of attack and
        the hover wingbeat loses its force rapidly, from
        $C_\psi = 1.43$ at hover to $0.90$ at $J = 0.4$ and
        essentially zero ($0.04$) by $J = 0.8$. Re-tuning recovers
        force ($1.17$ at $J = 0.4$, $1.00$ at $J = 0.8$) by
        \emph{reducing} $\psi_1$ to keep the section near its best
        angle of attack in the combined flow.
    \item \textbf{In-plane wind ($\chi$ near $\pm 90^\circ$)}: the
        mean-square relative velocity grows as the wind adds to the
        wing speed on one half-stroke faster than it subtracts on the
        other, and $C_\psi$ \emph{increases} strongly ($2.10$ at
        $J = 0.4$, $3.66$ at $J = 0.8$ at hover pitch). The hover
        wingbeat is already close to optimal here; re-tuning adds
        only a few percent.
    \item \textbf{Reverse axial inflow ($\chi$ near $180^\circ$)}: the
        inflow steepens the angle of attack, and the optimum saturates
        at the upper bound of the pitch amplitude range
        ($\psi_1 = 60^\circ$).
\end{itemize}
Between the in-plane and reverse-axial regimes, around
$\chi \approx \pm 110^\circ$ at high $J$, the optimal $\psi_1$
switches branches abruptly (from near-zero pitch amplitude, where
the wing behaves like a fixed-pitch blade in a dominant wind,
to the large-amplitude branch), visible as the sharp boundary in
panel (c).

For equilibrium, since the model has no body drag, steady flight at
any velocity requires the same force as hover: $F_a^* = 1$ directed
vertically. The magnitude condition is $q^* C_\psi = 1$, so panel (a)
divides the velocity plane into regions where the hover wingbeat
produces a force surplus that maneuvering can spend ($C_\psi$ above
its hover value, roughly the whole lower half-plane) and regions
where flight demands either a larger $q^*$ (faster or wider flapping)
or a re-tuned $\psi_1$ (the dashed $C_\psi = 1$ lobe around $\chi = 0$
marks where climbing flight with the reference $q^* = 1$ becomes
infeasible at hover pitch). The direction condition is taken up next:
at nonzero velocity the force direction $\beta$ is no longer a
function of $\psi_0$ alone but acquires a strong $(J, \chi)$
dependence (e.g.\ at $\chi = 90^\circ$, $J = 0.3$ the force tilts
by $-31^\circ$ at $\psi_0 = 0$), which is the central complication
for the maneuvering controller.

\subsection{Control for Maneuvering Flight}

Two features of the model shape the whole control problem. First, with
no body drag, steady straight flight at \emph{any} velocity needs the
same net force as hover---the weight, pointing up---so there is no
trim curve to chase with speed; ``cruise'' is just a re-trim of the
controls as the wing comes to see inflow, and maneuvering proper
(accelerating, changing flight path) is a transient excursion of the
force vector away from weight-up. Second, the cycle-averaged force is a
velocity-scheduled map $\vec{F}_a^*(\vec{u};\, \vec{v}_b)$ of the
controls $\vec{u}$, of which the hover controller of the previous
section is the $\vec{v}_b = 0$ slice. The maneuvering controller is
therefore the hover controller scheduled on velocity, and the new
content lives entirely in the velocity dependence already mapped in
\autoref{fig:velocity_cpsi}.

We keep $\delta_0 = 90^\circ$ (optimal everywhere) and use four
controls: the two hover handles $\phi_1$ (magnitude) and $\psi_0$
(direction), plus the two redundant degrees of freedom $\psi_1$ and
$\gamma_0$. We resolve the redundancy by minimizing the cycle-averaged
aerodynamic power---the operative form of the cost of endurance
$P^*$ of \autoref{sec:power}, which it equals up to the constant
weight-support normalization.

\subsubsection*{Power-Optimal Trim Across the Velocity Plane}

For straight flight at body velocity $\vec{v}_b$ we solve for the
power-optimal $\vec{u}$ producing weight-up, sweeping the redundant
$(\gamma_0, \psi_1)$ and trimming $(\phi_1, \psi_0)$ to the force at
each node. The result (\autoref{fig:man_trim}) sets up the controller.
The power-optimal stroke plane is \emph{horizontal} ($\gamma_0 = 0$)
for hover, forward flight and climb alike; only descent rewards a tilt.
In forward flight $\psi_0$ does the steering, swinging from $0$ to
$-54^\circ$ and saturating near its $-60^\circ$ bound by $J \approx
0.9$; $\psi_1$ re-tunes \emph{down} with inflow ($60^\circ$ to
$39^\circ$), as the velocity maps anticipated; and $\phi_1$ grows to
hold the magnitude. The cost of forward flight rises steeply and
monotonically (about ninefold by $J = 0.9$): with no induced-power
reduction modeled, the drag dissipation simply grows with the cube of
the relative speed, so there is no efficient cruise speed here. Vertical
flight is gentler. Climb stays feasible by re-tuning $\psi_1$ (the
infeasibility of the hover wingbeat at $\chi = 0$ is lifted once the
pitch is allowed to adapt), at an aerodynamic cost that is mild until
the climb work $\vec{v}_b \cdot \vec{F}_a^* = V_z W$ is added back to
give the true metabolic cost; in descent the wing approaches an
autorotative state, flapping little while the upward inflow carries the
load.

\subsubsection*{Directional Authority: $\psi_0$ vs.\ $\gamma_0$}

The previous section flagged that at velocity the force direction is no
longer set by $\psi_0$ alone. We can now compare the two directional
controls quantitatively. At hover $\psi_0$ is the better lever, tilting
the force by $1.25^\circ$ per degree against $1.00^\circ$ per degree for
$\gamma_0$, and it has its full range available. As forward speed grows,
the two trade places: $\psi_0$'s authority falls (to $0.80^\circ$ per
degree by $J = 0.8$) while $\gamma_0$'s rises (to $1.26^\circ$),
crossing near $J \approx 0.5$. Worse, the $\psi_0$ \emph{trim} itself
marches toward its bound (to $-55^\circ$, leaving $5^\circ$ of headroom
by $J = 0.8$), so it runs out of steering range just as it loses
sensitivity. This is why we hold $\gamma_0$ horizontal at low speed,
where $\psi_0$ is both more effective and unsaturated, and reserve
$\gamma_0$ as the directional control for fast forward flight, where
$\psi_0$ can no longer do the job.

\subsubsection*{Closed-Loop Maneuvers}

The controller is a first-order velocity tracker with feedforward,
$\vec{a}_\text{des} = \vec{a}_\text{ref} + K_v (\vec{v}_\text{ref} -
\vec{v}_b)$, whose required force $\vec{F}_\text{des} =
\vec{a}_\text{des} - \vec{g}$ is realized by dynamic inversion of the
cycle-averaged map: hold $\gamma_0 = 0$, re-optimize $\psi_1$ for power,
and trim $(\phi_1, \psi_0)$ to $\vec{F}_\text{des}$ at the current
velocity. As at hover, the control is updated once per wingbeat---an
order of magnitude below $\omega^*$---held across the beat, and the
\emph{unsteady} instantaneous force is integrated, so the run is a
genuine test of the cycle-averaged design.

\autoref{fig:man_ctrl} shows two maneuvers. In the forward dash the body
tracks an accelerate--cruise--decelerate velocity command to $J \approx
0.5$ and back, with only a small within-cycle ripple at cruise
(standard deviation $\approx 0.018$ in $v_x^*$) and a peak tracking
error of $0.19$ confined to the hardest acceleration phase. In the
pull-up the velocity command rotates from forward to vertical, and the
body follows it through a smooth quarter-turn in the sagittal plane,
with $\psi_0$ steering from $-42^\circ$ to near $0$ as the flight path
swings into the climb (peak speed error $0.04$). Both confirm that the
velocity-scheduled, power-optimal inversion controls maneuvering flight
across the regimes where the inflow strongly reshapes the force map.
