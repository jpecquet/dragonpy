\section{Summary}%
\label{sec:intro}

\begin{itemize}
    \item A 2D point-mass model with quasi-steady blade-element wings reduces the
    wingbeat-averaged aerodynamic force to a product $F_a^* = q^* C_{F^*}$: a
    magnitude scale $q^*$ set by flapping amplitude and frequency, and a force
    coefficient $C_{F^*}$ set by the wing-pitch kinematics. The separation also
    holds in moving flight.
    \item Two control handles follow: the flapping amplitude $\phi_1$ sets force
    magnitude and the mean pitch $\psi_0$ sets its direction. A proportional
    controller running an order of magnitude below the wingbeat frequency holds a
    stable hover across the full morphological parameter range.
    \item Within this quasi-steady model the inclined hover stroke plane carries no
    efficiency benefit; it is a force-steering choice, realized through a nonzero
    $\psi_0$, rather than an energetic one.
    \item For flight along the stroke-plane normal (axial inflow) the cycle-mean
    force stays on the normal and the entire velocity dependence collapses onto the
    advance ratio $J$: the force coefficient falls with $J$ and the
    force-maximizing pitch amplitude retunes downward.
    \item The maneuvering controller extends the hover controller by scheduling the
    stroke-plane angle to meet the inflow axially and slaving the pitch amplitude to
    $J$. In forward flight this keeps $\psi_0$ small and unsaturated at a near-flat
    power cost, far cheaper than holding the stroke plane horizontal.
    \item In unsteady closed-loop simulation the controller tracks accelerations, a
    pull-up, a fully reversing flight loop, and pure-pursuit prey capture; its
    binding limit is sustained, dynamic descent.
\end{itemize}

\section{Model Description}%
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Summary}

We model a dragonfly-like insect with two pairs of wings.
The sagittal plane is aligned with the fixed frame $(x,z)$
plane, where $z$ points opposite the direction of gravity.
The wings in each pair beat symmetrically about the sagittal
plane, and there is zero initial velocity or wind component
normal to the sagittal plane, so the motion is constrained
to the $(x,z)$ plane, rendering the problem two-dimensional.
A subsequent strong simplification is that the body is reduced
to a point mass, so there is no coupling between body pitch
and wing kinematics. In this manner we can focus entirely on
the translational dynamics without concern for flight stability.
The dynamical model is nondimensionalized using the body length $L$,
the body mass $m$, and the gravitational time scale $\sqrt{L/g}$.
The resulting dimensionless parameters, along with the wing kinematics
angular parameters, are listed in \autoref{tab:nondim_params}.
Dimensionless quantities are denoted by an asterisk ($*$).

\subsection{Aerodynamic Force Derivation}

Each wing is modeled as a single, massless plate element of
area $A_w$ with its aerodynamic center at the $\frac23-$span station,
$r = \frac23 L_w$, whose
lift and drag coefficients are prescribed by
functions of the instantaneous angle of attack $\alpha$.
These simplified functions, given by \autoref{eq:cl}
and \autoref{eq:cd}, nevertheless capture the essential phenomena.
Parameters for the wing kinematics are defined in \autoref{fig:kinematics1}
and \autoref{fig:kinematics2}
for a single right-hand-side wing. Since the wings of each pair
are assumed to beat symmetrically about the sagittal plane,
the left-hand-side wing kinematics can be obtained by mirroring about
the $(x,z)$ plane.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=5in]{../figures/kinematics1.png}
    \caption{Wing stroke geometry}\label{fig:kinematics1}
\end{figure}

Each wing element sweeps along an arc of radius $r = \frac23 L_w$
that lies inside the stroke plane, with normal vector
$\vec{n} = \cos \gamma \vec{z} - \sin \gamma \vec{x}$,
where $\gamma$ is the stroke plane inclination angle
relative to the horizontal. The sweep angle $\phi$ about $\vec{n}$
is defined relative to the side vector $\vec{y}$, positive
counterclockwise about $\vec{n}$.
At any point along the wing stroke, the wing velocity
relative to the body is
\begin{align}
    \vec{v}_{w/b} = \frac23 L_w \dot{\phi} \vec{s}
\end{align}
with $\vec{s} = \cos\phi \left( \cos \gamma \vec{x} + \sin \gamma \vec{z} \right) -\sin\phi \vec{y}$.
The sweep angle $\phi$ for wing pair $i$ is prescribed by
\begin{align}
    \phi^{(i)}(t) = \phi_1 \sin \left(\omega t - \sigma_0^{(i)}\right)
\end{align}
The phase parameter $\sigma_0^{(i)}$ is set to $0$ for
the forewing pair, and to $\sigma_0$ for the hindwing pair.
Both wing pairs beat at the same frequency $\omega$ and
with the same amplitude $\phi_1$. In addition to the sweeping
motion, the wing rotates about its own spanwise axis.
We refer to this as the wing pitch. It is most easily
described in the $(\vec{s},\vec{n})$ plane.
\autoref{fig:kinematics2} shows the pitch angle $\psi$
defined relative to the stroke plane normal $\vec{n}$.
$\psi$ is prescribed for wing pair $i$ by
\begin{align}
    \psi^{(i)}(t) = \psi_0 + \psi_1 \sin \left(\omega t - \delta_0 - \sigma_0^{(i)}\right)
\end{align}
Unlike for the sweep angle $\phi$, the mean pitch angle $\psi_0$
may be nonzero, and can be used to redirect the mean aerodynamic
force vector, as we will see.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=6in]{../figures/kinematics2.png}
    \caption{Wing pitch geometry and angle of attack for (a) $\vec{v}_w\cdot\vec{s} > \vec{c}\cdot\vec{s}$ and (b) $\vec{v}_w\cdot\vec{s} < \vec{c}\cdot\vec{s}$}\label{fig:kinematics2}
\end{figure}

It is convenient to define the chord direction vector
$\vec{c} = \cos\psi \vec{n} - \sin\psi \vec{s}$.
The angle of attack $\alpha$ is the angle between
$\vec{c}$ and $\vec{v}_w$, the projection in the
$(\vec{s},\vec{n})$ plane of the wing velocity relative
to the air. Care has to be taken as to the sign of $\alpha$,
as evidenced by the two panels in \autoref{fig:kinematics2}.
When $\vec{v}_w \cdot \vec{s} > \vec{c} \cdot \vec{s}$, $\alpha$ has the correct sign
when defined from $\vec{v}_w$ to $\vec{c}$. When $\vec{v}_w \cdot \vec{s} < \vec{c} \cdot \vec{s}$,
it has the correct sign when defined from $\vec{c}$ to $\vec{v}_w$.
The following expression gives the correct sign:
\begin{align}
    \alpha = \sin^{-1} \left[\left( \frac{\vec{v}_w}{v_w} \times \vec{c} \right) \cdot \left( \vec{s} \times \vec{n} \right)\right]
\end{align}
Lift and drag coefficients are then obtained by
\begin{align}
    C_L(\alpha) &= C_{L,0} \sin 2\alpha \label{eq:cl} \\
    C_D(\alpha) &= C_{D,0} \cos^2 \alpha + C_{D,\frac{\pi}{2}} \sin^2 \alpha \label{eq:cd}
\end{align}
with $C_{L,0} = 1.5$, $C_{D,0} = 0.1$, $C_{D,\frac{\pi}{2}} = 2$,
as suggested by numerical simulations and experiments
across the full range of angle of attack~\cite{wang2004, wang2008}.
The lift and drag vectors are, respectively,
\begin{align}
    \vec{d} &= -\frac{\vec{v}_w}{v_w} \\
    \vec{\ell} &= \vec{d} \times \left( \vec{s} \times \vec{n} \right)
\end{align}
And the aerodynamic force of a single wing is
\begin{align}
    \vec{F}_{a,w} &= \frac12 \rho_\text{air} A_w v_w^2 \left( C_D \vec{d} + C_L \vec{\ell} \right)
\end{align}


\subsection{Nondimensionalization}

The flying insect is subjected to gravity and the aerodynamic
force on its wings. Body drag is neglected. The resulting equation of motion is
\begin{align}
    m \left(\ddot{x}\vec{x} + \ddot{z}\vec{z}\right) &= \frac12 \rho_\text{air} A_w \sum_i^{N_w} \left[ v_w^2 \left( C_D \vec{d} + C_L \vec{\ell} \right) \right]^{(i)} - mg\vec{z}
\end{align}
We nondimensionalize it using the body length $L$,
the body mass $m$, and the gravitational time scale $\sqrt{L/g}$.
The resulting dimensionless parameters, along with the already
dimensionless wing angles, are summarized in \autoref{tab:nondim_params}.
Ranges for the parameters
$L_w^*$, $A^* / m^*$, and $\omega^*$ were obtained from
the morphological data in~\cite{wakeling1997} and the wingbeat
frequency data in~\cite{aracheloff2024} for \emph{Anisoptera}. The
resulting dimensionless equation of motion is
\begin{align}
    \ddot{x}^*\vec{x} + \ddot{z}^*\vec{z} &= \frac12 \frac{A^*}{m^*} \left( \frac23 L_w^* \phi_1 \omega^* \right)^2 \frac{1}{N_w} \sum_i^{N_w} \left[ v_w^{*2} \left( C_D \vec{d} + C_L \vec{\ell} \right) \right]^{(i)} - 1\vec{z}
\end{align}

\section{Two Analytical Cases}

\subsection{Stationary Flight}\label{sec:analytical-hover}

\subsubsection{Wingbeat-averaged Aerodynamic Force}\label{sec:hover-force}

In stationary flight, or \emph{hovering}, the wingbeat-averaged
aerodynamic force vector is opposed to and equal in magnitude to
the weight vector. There are residual oscillations of the body
due to fluctuations in the aerodynamic force over a wingbeat cycle,
and inertial coupling between the body and wings (not modeled here).
Assuming the corresponding body velocities are small compared to the
characteristic wing velocity $\frac23 L_w^* \phi_1 \omega^*$, we can approximate
the mean aerodynamic force analytically by setting $\dot{x}^* = \dot{z}^* = 0$.
As a result, the angle of attack is a simple function of the pitch
angle $\psi$ and the instantaneous sign of the flapping velocity:
\begin{align}
    \alpha = - \frac{\dot{\phi}}{|\dot{\phi}|} \left[ \frac{\pi}{2} - \psi \right]
\end{align}
After making use of the appropriate trigonometric identities, this
gives the lift and drag coefficients
\begin{align}
    C_L &= -\frac{\dot{\phi}}{|\dot{\phi}|} C_{L,0} \sin 2\psi \\
    C_D &= C_{D,0} \sin^2 \psi + C_{D,\frac{\pi}{2}} \cos^2 \psi \nonumber \\
        &= \frac{C_{D,\frac{\pi}{2}} + C_{D,0}}{2} + \frac{C_{D,\frac{\pi}{2}} - C_{D,0}}{2} \cos 2\psi
\end{align}
The aerodynamic force normal to the stroke plane (along $\vec{n}$)
is pure lift, and the force within the stroke plane (along $\vec{s}$)
is pure drag. Denoting these two components as $F_{w,n}^*$ and $F_{w,s}^*$,
respectively, they are
\begin{align}
    F_{w,n}^* &= \frac12 \frac{A_w^*}{m^*} \left( \frac23 L_w^* \dot{\phi} \right)^2 C_L \\
    F_{w,s}^* &= - \frac{\dot{\phi}}{|\dot{\phi}|} \frac12 \frac{A_w^*}{m^*} \left( \frac23 L_w^* \dot{\phi} \right)^2 C_D
\end{align}
At this point, it is useful to linearize, in a sense, about the $\phi = 0$
position, by treating $\vec{s}$ as a fixed vector, parallel to the $(x,z)$
plane. This is a common modeling choice, see \emph{e.g.} \cite{wetherbee2026}.
The effect of the simplification is that force is increasingly
overestimated as the wingbeat amplitude $\phi_1$ increases. The effect is
small for reasonable values of $\phi_1$.
Note the numerical model retains the moving $\vec{s}$ (wing beats along an arc,
not a straight line) and we only perform the simplification for the analytical
derivation, so that the time integral depends on $\phi_1$ only as a
simple scaling factor. Note further that the simplification
has no effect on the mean if the wing pitch is symmetric
for both halves of the stroke, since $\langle F_s^* \rangle$ would be zero regardless.

With the above simplification made, we introduce the
sweep kinematics $\phi(t^*) = \phi_1 \sin \omega^* t^*$,
giving $\dot{\phi} = \phi_1 \omega^* \cos \omega^* t^*$.
With the notation $\tau^* = \omega^* t^*$, we evaluate the
wingbeat cycle integral $\langle\cdot\rangle = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^{2\pi} (\cdot) d\tau^*$.
An important question is how to deal with the sign flips
due to the $\dot{\phi}/|\dot{\phi}|$ factors appearing in both $F_n^*$
(through $C_L$) and $F_s^*$. We can actually fold them
into the "velocity squared" factors as follows
\begin{align}
    \frac{\dot{\phi}}{|\dot\phi|} \dot{\phi}^2 &= \dot{\phi} |\dot{\phi}| \nonumber \\
                                              &= \phi_1^2 \omega^{*2} \cos\tau^* |\cos\tau^*|
\end{align}
It is also convenient to introduce the wingbeat amplitude
shorthand $s_0^* = \frac23 L_w^* \phi_1$. Evaluating the integrals,
and multiplying by the number of wings ($A_w^*$ becomes $A^*$),
we obtain the total cycle-averaged aerodynamic force components
\begin{align}
    \langle F_n^* \rangle &= \frac12 \frac{A^*}{m^*} \left( s_0^* \omega^* \right)^2 \langle C_L |\cos \tau^*|^2 \rangle \nonumber \\
    &= -\frac12 \frac{A^*}{m^*} \left( s_0^* \omega^* \right)^2 C_{L,0} \langle \sin 2\psi\; \cos \tau^* |\cos \tau^*| \rangle \\
    \langle F_s^* \rangle &= -\frac12 \frac{A^*}{m^*} \left( s_0^* \omega^* \right)^2 \langle C_D \cos \tau^* |\cos \tau^*| \rangle \nonumber \\
    &= -\frac12 \frac{A^*}{m^*} \left( s_0^* \omega^* \right)^2 \frac{C_{D,\frac\pi2} - C_{D,0}}{2}\, \langle \cos 2\psi\; \cos \tau^* |\cos \tau^*| \rangle
\end{align}
Note the constant drag term vanishes because $\langle \cos \tau^* |\cos \tau^*| \rangle = 0$.
The expressions feature a common $\frac12 \frac{A^*}{m^*} \left( s_0^* \omega^* \right)^2$
factor, and a pitch- and aerodynamic-coefficient-dependent
factor, which is lift-based for $\langle F_n^* \rangle$,
and drag-based for $\langle F_s^* \rangle$. At this point,
the only kinematics assumption we have made is sinusoidal
flapping of the wings, while $\psi$ remains generic.
First, we can recover the analytical result of~\cite{wetherbee2026}
for symmetric flapping with $\psi = -\frac{\pi}{4}$ for
the $+\vec{s}$ stroke, and $\psi = +\frac{\pi}{4}$ for the
$-\vec{s}$ stroke. Noting that $\cos \tau^* > 0$ for the $+\vec{s}$
stroke and $< 0$ for the $-\vec{s}$ stroke, and that
$\langle \cos^2\tau^* \rangle = \frac12$, we recover the result
\begin{align}
    \langle F_n^* \rangle &= \frac14 \frac{A^*}{m^*} \left( s_0^* \omega^* \right)^2 C_{L,0} \\
    \langle F_s^* \rangle &= 0 
\end{align}
We could obtain this result with our numerical
model if we were to replace the sine wave with a
square wave, and set $\psi_0 = 0$, $\psi_1 = \frac{\pi}{4}$.
In reality, dragonfly wing pitch kinematics follow
something between a sine wave and a square wave (\emph{i.e.}
a somewhat flattened sine wave). We stick to a sine wave
here so as not to introduce higher harmonics (\cite{azuma1985}) or a "flattening"
coefficient (\cite{berman2007}), while keeping the more physical non-instantaneous
stroke reversal (would be instantaneous with a square wave).
To look at the effect of the pitch parameters on
the force magnitude in the stroke plane and stroke-plane-normal
directions, we introduce
\begin{align}
    q^* &= \frac14 \frac{A^*}{m^*} \left( s_0^* \omega^* \right)^2 \\
    C_{F^*,n} &= -2 C_{L,0} \langle \sin 2\psi\; \cos \tau^* |\cos \tau^*| \rangle \label{eq:cfn} \\
    C_{F^*,s} &= -\left(C_{D,\frac\pi2} - C_{D,0}\right) \langle \cos 2\psi\; \cos \tau^* |\cos \tau^*| \rangle \label{eq:cfs}
\end{align}
such that $\langle F_n^* \rangle = C_{F^*,n} q^*$
and $\langle F_s^* \rangle = C_{F^*,s} q^*$.
It is also convenient to introduce the overall magnitude
$C_{F^*} = \sqrt{C_{F^*,n}^2 + C_{F^*,s}^2}$.

\subsubsection{Power Efficiency}\label{sec:hover-power}

In addition to the mean aerodynamic force, another
quantity of interest is the mean power developed over
a wingbeat. Because we assume the wings are massless,
power is only due to the work of the aerodynamic force.
In the hovering case, only the drag force does work,
because the lift force is strictly normal to $\vec{s}$.
It follows the (dimensionless) wingbeat-averaged power is
\begin{align}
    \langle P^* \rangle &= \langle|F_s^* s_0^* \omega^* \cos\tau^*|\rangle \nonumber \\
        &= \frac12 \frac{A^*}{m^*} \left( s_0^* \omega^* \right)^3 \langle C_D |\cos \tau^*|^3 \rangle
\end{align}
Following the derivation of the \emph{cost of endurance}
in~\cite{wang2008}, we can normalize the above by introducing
the quantity $u_\text{ref} = \sqrt{2mg/\rho A}$, more specifically
its dimensionless form $u_\text{ref}^* = \sqrt{2/\frac{A^*}{m^*}}$, giving
\begin{align}
    \frac{\langle P^* \rangle}{u_\text{ref}^*} &= \frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}} \left[ \frac{A^*}{m^*} \left( s_0^* \omega^* \right)^2 \right]^{3/2} \langle C_D |\cos \tau^*|^3 \rangle \nonumber \\
                                               &= 2\sqrt{2} \left(q^*\right)^{3/2} \langle C_D |\cos \tau^*|^3 \rangle
\end{align}
When hovering, the wingbeat-average net aerodynamic force
$C_{F^*} q^* = 1 \implies q^* = 1/C_{F^*}$. We introduce the
hovering power coefficient
\begin{align}
    C_{P^*} &= 2\sqrt{2} \frac{\langle C_D |\cos \tau^*|^3 \rangle}{C_{F^*}^{3/2}}
\end{align}
which is analogous to the steady value  $C_D / C_L^{3/2}$.
The velocity-weighted drag coefficient in the numerator
plays the role of $C_D$, and the force coefficient $C_{F^*}$
in the denominator, which is, in general, part lift and part drag, plays
the role of $C_L$. To be more specific, $C_{F^*}$ is lift-only
as long as the wing stroke is symmetric ($\psi_0 = 0$), which, for hovering,
requires the stroke plane to be horizontal. Hovering at an inclined
stroke plane requires, as we will see in \autoref{sec:optimal-hover}, $\psi_0 \neq 0$,
resulting in drag contributing to $C_{F^*}$.

\subsubsection{Force-Maximizing and Power-Minimizing Hover Kinematics}\label{sec:optimal-hover}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{../figures/pitch_efficiency.light.png}
    \caption{Dependence of $C_{F^*}$ on pitch parameters at zero body velocity. Maximum shown as white dots.}\label{fig:cf}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{../figures/pitch_power.light.png}
    \caption{Dependence of $C_{P^*}$ on pitch parameters for hovering. Minimum shown as white dots.}\label{fig:cp}
\end{figure}

$C_L(\alpha)$ and $C_D(\alpha)$ being fixed, the force
and power coefficients $C_{F^*}$ and $C_{P^*}$ defined in
\autoref{sec:hover-force} and \autoref{sec:hover-power}
respectively, depend only on the pitch parameters $\psi_0$,
$\psi_1$, and $\delta_0$. We visualize these coefficients
across two 2D slices, one at $\psi_0 = 0^\circ$, and one at $\delta_0 = 90^\circ$,
in \autoref{fig:cf} and \autoref{fig:cp}. The optimal
values of each coefficient (maximum $C_{F^*}$ and minimum
$C_{P^*}$), indicated with white dots, both occur at $\psi_0 = 0^\circ$ and $\delta_0 = 90^\circ$,
with different values of the pitching amplitude $\psi_1$.
Unsurprisingly, the $C_{F^*}$ maximum occurs close to $\psi_1 = 45^\circ$, which
maximizes lift, while the $C_{P^*}$ minimum occurs close to $\psi_1 = 90^\circ$,
which minimizes drag.
To be exact, there are also maxima/minima at $\psi_0 = \pm90^\circ$.
They are interesting, but do not seem to correspond to typical
dragonfly wingbeat patterns, so we disregard them here.

An important consequence of the maximum and minimum
occuring at $\psi_0 = 0^\circ$ is that energy-efficient
wing pitch occurs for a symmetric wingbeat, which
results in a net mean force normal to the stroke plane,
necessitating the use of a horizontal stroke plane.
In other words, our quasi-steady lift and drag model
is unable to account for the efficiency advantage
of the dragonfly inclined stroke plane evidenced in
other work~\cite{wang2004, wang2008}.
Since we want the model kinematics to still
resemble dragonfly flight, we will impose a nonzero
hover stroke plane angle $\gamma$ (see \autoref{sec:controller}),
independent of efficiency considerations for the present model.
This will require the wingbeat-averaged force vector
to be angled relative to the stroke plane normal by an
amount $-\gamma$. As we will now see, this can be achieved
with nonzero $\psi_0$. Let us denote the angle of the force vector to the stroke plane
normal $\vec{n}$ as $\beta$. Since the wingbeat-averaged force is
$\langle \vec{F}^* \rangle = q^* \left( C_{F^*,n} \vec{n} + C_{F^*,s} \vec{s} \right)$,
$\beta$ follows from the $C_{F^*,n}$ and $C_{F^*,s}$ coefficients
(\autoref{eq:cfn} and \autoref{eq:cfs}) as
\begin{align}
    \beta = -\operatorname{atan2}\left( C_{F^*,s},\, C_{F^*,n} \right), \label{eq:beta}
\end{align}
taken positive counterclockwise from $\vec{n}$ (viewed with $\vec{x}$
pointing right and $\vec{z}$ up). Thus $\beta = 0$ for a force
along $\vec{n}$, and $\beta = -\gamma$ aims the mean force along the
vertical for hover at stroke-plane angle $\gamma$.
\autoref{fig:cf_components} maps the two components over the pitch
plane. $C_{F^*,n}$ is largest for a near-symmetric stroke, whereas
$C_{F^*,s}$ vanishes at $\psi_0 = 0$ and grows with $|\psi_0|$,
supplying the off-normal force direction that an inclined-stroke
hover requires.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{../figures/cf_components.light.png}
    \caption{Stroke-normal (lift) component $C_{F^*,n}$ (a) and in-stroke (drag) component $C_{F^*,s}$ (b) of the force coefficient over the pitch plane at $\delta_0 = 90^\circ$. The force direction quadrant in the $(\vec{s},\vec{n})$ plane can be inferred from the signs.}\label{fig:cf_components}
\end{figure}

We further show that $\beta$ depends
on $\psi_0$ alone, independent of the pitch amplitude $\psi_1$ and the phase
magnitude $|\delta_0|$. The weight $\cos\tau^*|\cos\tau^*|$ in \autoref{eq:cfn}
and \autoref{eq:cfs} is antisymmetric under $\tau^* \to \tau^* + \pi$, a shift
that also maps $\psi \to 2\psi_0 - \psi$ (since
$\sin(\tau^* + \pi - \delta_0) = -\sin(\tau^* - \delta_0)$). Splitting each
cycle average over the two half-strokes and applying the sum-to-product
identities gives
\begin{align}
    \langle \sin 2\psi\; \cos\tau^*|\cos\tau^*| \rangle &= \phantom{-}\cos 2\psi_0\; K, \\
    \langle \cos 2\psi\; \cos\tau^*|\cos\tau^*| \rangle &= -\sin 2\psi_0\; K,
\end{align}
where the factor
\begin{align}
    K = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_0^\pi \sin\!\left[ 2\psi_1 \sin(\tau^* - \delta_0) \right] \cos\tau^* |\cos\tau^*| \; d\tau^*
\end{align}
contains the entire $\psi_1$ and $|\delta_0|$ dependence. It enters $C_{F^*,n}$
and $C_{F^*,s}$ as a common factor and so cancels in their ratio. Only its sign remains,
fixed by the sign of $\delta_0$, flipping the force by $180^\circ$.
With \autoref{eq:beta} this yields the closed form
\begin{align}
    \beta = \operatorname{atan2}\!\left[ \left(C_{D,\frac\pi2} - C_{D,0}\right) \sin 2\psi_0,\; 2 C_{L,0} \cos 2\psi_0 \right] \label{eq:beta_psi0}
\end{align}
for $\delta_0 > 0$. In particular $\beta = 0,\, 90^\circ,\, 180^\circ$ at
$\psi_0 = 0,\, 45^\circ,\, 90^\circ$, and near hover
$\beta \approx \frac{C_{D,\frac\pi2} - C_{D,0}}{C_{L,0}}\, \psi_0 \approx 1.3\,\psi_0$.
The full numerical model retains the moving stroke tangent ($\vec{s}$ not constant)
and departs from
\autoref{eq:beta_psi0} by at most ${\sim}1^\circ$ over the $\psi_1$ and
$|\delta_0|$ ranges, so the independence is exact in the analytical model and
very nearly so in practice.
We show $\beta(\psi_0)$ in \autoref{fig:beta_vs_psi0}
along with the maximum $C_{F^*}$ at a given $\psi_0$.

\subsection{Stroke-Plane-Normal Flight}

\subsubsection{Rationale}

Dragonflies have been observed to fly with their
stroke plane normal $\vec{n}$ approximately aligned to
the direction of travel~\cite{azuma1985}. We treat that
aligned configuration here, with the body advancing along
$\vec{n}$, and use the analysis for controller design in
\autoref{sec:general-controller}.


\subsubsection{Wingbeat-averaged Aerodynamic Force}\label{sec:spn-force}

For flight along the stroke-plane normal the only velocity
scale beyond the wing speed is the body speed $v_b^*$, which we
carry through the advance ratio
\begin{align*}
    J = \frac{v_b^*}{s_0^* \omega^*},
\end{align*}
i.e.\ the body speed measured against the wing-speed scale
$s_0^* \omega^*$. This choice has two virtues. First, the
$\gamma$-invariance of the force magnitude carries over:
$|\vec{F}_a^*|$ is unchanged when the stroke plane and the
velocity vector are rotated together (verified numerically to
machine precision), so a single map in $J$ covers normal flight
at every stroke-plane angle. Second, scaling the speed with
$s_0^* \omega^*$ keeps every velocity in the problem proportional
to the wing speed, which preserves the $q^*$ magnitude scaling
of \autoref{sec:hover-force}: the body motion can only enter
through the coefficient, which becomes
$C_{F^*}(\psi_0, \psi_1, \delta_0;\, J)$.

This presumes the separation $F_a^* = q^* C_{F^*}$ of
\autoref{sec:hover-force} survives at nonzero velocity. It does,
exactly, for flight along the normal: the body velocity has no
component along the in-plane sweep direction $\vec{e}_\parallel$,
so the sweep term in $v_s$ is untouched by the body motion,
\begin{align}
    v_s = \tfrac23 L_w \dot\phi, \qquad v_n = \text{const},
\end{align}
and the cycle geometry depends on the wingbeat amplitude only
through $s_0^* \omega^*$. (Evaluating the scatter of $F_a^*/q^*$
over random draws of $(\frac{A^*}{m^*}, \omega^*, \phi_1, L_w^*)$
at fixed pitch and fixed $J$ returns a residual that is exactly
zero.) The wing therefore sees an oscillating sweep superposed
on a uniform normal inflow---the classical heaving-plus-inflow
kinematics of a blade element---and the angle of attack splits
additively into the pitch and the relative-wind angle,
\begin{align}
    \alpha(\tau) = \psi(\tau) + \theta(\tau), \qquad
    \theta(\tau) = \operatorname{atan2}(v_s, v_n), \label{eq:alpha_inflow}
\end{align}
with $\tau = \omega^* t^*$. As the inflow vanishes ($J \to 0$) the
relative-wind angle returns to $\theta \to \pm\tfrac\pi2$ and the
hover result of \autoref{sec:hover-force} is recovered.

Writing the sweep-speed amplitude as $u = s_0^* \omega^*$ and the normal
inflow through $J$, we have $v_s = u\cos\tau$, $v_n = J u$ for a body
advancing along the cycle-mean force, and $v_w = u\sqrt{\cos^2\tau + J^2}$.
Substituting into the lift and drag of \autoref{eq:cl} and \autoref{eq:cd}
and cycle-averaging in the small-$\phi$ limit---where, as in
\autoref{sec:hover-force}, the stroke basis is frozen and the average of
the vector equals the vector of the averages---gives the moving-flight
coefficient
\begin{align}
    C_{F^*}(\psi_0, \psi_1, \delta_0;\, J)
        &= 2\sqrt{\langle \tilde F_s\rangle^2 + \langle \tilde F_n\rangle^2}, \\
    \tilde F_n &= \sqrt{\cos^2\tau + J^2}\,\bigl(C_{L,0}\sin 2\alpha\,\cos\tau - J\,C_D\bigr), \\
    \tilde F_s &= -\sqrt{\cos^2\tau + J^2}\,\bigl(C_D \cos\tau + J\,C_{L,0}\sin 2\alpha\bigr),
\end{align}
where $\tilde F_s, \tilde F_n$ are the stroke-frame components in units
of $\tfrac12 \rho_\text{air} A_w u^2$ and $\alpha$ is given by
\autoref{eq:alpha_inflow}. The entire velocity dependence has collapsed
onto the single parameter $J$.

A parity argument sharpens the picture, paralleling the symmetric-pitch
result of \autoref{sec:hover-force}. For symmetric pitch ($\psi_0 = 0$,
$\delta_0 = \tfrac\pi2$) the substitution $\tau \to \pi - \tau$ sends
$v_s \to -v_s$, $\psi \to -\psi$, and hence $\alpha \to -\alpha$ at fixed
$v_n$, so the $\tilde F_s$ integrand is odd and
$\langle \tilde F_s\rangle = 0$: the cycle-mean force lies
\emph{exactly} along the stroke-plane normal at all advance ratios
(verified numerically to $0.01^\circ$). The aligned wingbeat therefore
produces a force purely along the flight axis; redirecting it---for
instance to support weight away from vertical flight---requires breaking
the symmetry through the mean pitch $\psi_0$, the same lever used in
hover. The coefficient then reduces to the single integral
\begin{align}
    C_{F^*}(J) = 2\left| \Bigl\langle \sqrt{\cos^2\tau + J^2}\,
        \bigl( C_{L,0}\sin 2\alpha\,\cos\tau - J\,C_D \bigr) \Bigr\rangle \right|,
\end{align}
which returns to the hover value as $J\to0$ and decreases monotonically
with $J$ as the growing inflow detunes the angle of attack and bleeds off
force---the wing losing authority as it advances into its own induced
direction. At the hover-optimal pitch the coefficient falls from
$C_{F^*} = 1.43$ at hover to $1.21$ at $J = 0.2$ and $0.90$ at $J = 0.4$
(\autoref{fig:axial_cpsi}).

The pitch amplitude that maximizes $C_{F^*}$ retunes with the inflow,
decreasing from the hover optimum ($\psi_1 \approx 50^\circ$) toward
$\psi_1 \approx 20^\circ$ at $J = 0.8$: the wing feathers less as the steady
normal inflow takes over from the sweep in setting the angle of attack,
which partly recovers the force lost at fixed pitch (to $1.17$ at
$J = 0.4$). We track the lift-producing optimum here; at large $\psi_1$ a
second branch of nominally higher $|\vec{F}_a^*|$ appears in which the
feathered wing is carried backward by the inflow, with cycle-mean force
opposed to $\vec{n}$, and is discarded as it cannot support weight. This
$\psi_1^{\max}(J)$ schedule is what the generalized controller of
\autoref{sec:general-controller} slaves to.

\subsubsection{Power-Optimal Trim}

For straight flight along the normal at body velocity $\vec{v}_b$ we
solve for the power-optimal sweep $\vec{u}$ producing weight-up, sweeping
the redundant $(\gamma, \psi_1)$ and trimming $(\phi_1, \psi_0)$ to the
force at each speed. The vertical-flight result (\autoref{fig:man_trim}(a))
sets up the controller. The power-optimal stroke plane is
\emph{horizontal} ($\gamma = 0$) for hover and climb alike; only descent
rewards a tilt. Climb stays feasible by re-tuning $\psi_1$---the
infeasibility of the hover wingbeat is lifted once the pitch is allowed
to adapt---at an aerodynamic cost that is mild until the climb work
$\vec{v}_b \cdot \vec{F}_a^* = V_z W$ is added back to give the true
metabolic cost; in descent the wing approaches an autorotative state,
flapping little while the upward inflow carries the load. The off-normal
forward-flight schedule of \autoref{fig:man_trim}(b)---where $\psi_0$
takes over the steering---belongs to the generalized controller of
\autoref{sec:general-controller}.

\section{Controller Design}\label{sec:controller}

\subsection{Hover Controller}

\subsubsection{Design Principle}

Insights from \autoref{sec:analytical-hover} suggest a few possible ways to achieve
hover control. Candidates for control variables can be split into
two groups: those that control force magnitude ($\phi_1, \omega^*$ through
$q^*$ and $\psi_1, \delta_0$ through $C_{F^*}$), and those that control force
direction ($\gamma$, $\psi_0$). Note this is not such a clean
split, because $\psi_0$ also changes force magnitude through $C_{F^*}$,
just more weakly than it changes direction. For simplicity, we
would rather consider $\omega^*$ to be a fixed parameter, so we will exclude it
at least for now.
Since we also do not fully understand the factors
that set the hovering stroke plane angle, we will also
consider $\gamma$ to be fixed for now (later on, we
compare the effectiveness of $\gamma$ and $\psi_0$
as directional control variables).
In addition, it is beneficial to keep $C_{F^*}$ at or near its maximum
value, which sets $\psi_1$ and $\delta_0$. This leaves $\phi_1$ (or $s_0^*$)
for magnitude and $\psi_0$ for direction. At equilibrium,
$\psi_0$ will be such that $\beta = -\gamma$ (force is vertical),
and $\phi_1$ such that $F_a^* = 1$. \autoref{tab:hover_psi0} shows
the required value of $\psi_0$ for different $\gamma$,
for a reference configuration $L_w^* = 0.75$, $\frac{A^*}{m^*} = 0.6$,
and $\omega^* = 14$. Different values of these parameters impose
a different $\phi_1$ for hover, which results in small
changes in $\psi_0$ up to a few percent.

    \centering
    \caption{Mean pitch for hover at various stroke plane angles for reference configuration}\label{tab:hover_psi0}
    \begin{tabular}{lll}\toprule

        $0^\circ$ & $0^\circ$ & $1.43$ \\

$\phi_1$ is set by
\begin{align*}
    \phi_1^\text{hover} = 3 \left( L_w^* \omega^* \sqrt{C_{F^*}^\text{max}(\psi_0) \frac{A^*}{m^*}} \right)^{-1}
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Departure from Wingbeat-Averaged Picture}

Before designing a controller, we look at how the actual, 
unsteady hover dynamics differ from the wingbeat-averaged picture
we have been working with so far. Indeed the body velocity
will not be exactly zero throughout, and will oscillate
according to the fluctuations of the aerodynamic force
over a wingbeat cycle. There is an additional effect due to
small but nonzero mass of the wings, which will make the
body bob up and down in the direction of the flapping motion.
We have neglected this in our work so far. In this part we will
evaluate the relative importance of the two effects on the
body oscillations.

A key parameter here is the fore/hind wing phase shift $\sigma_0$.
We expect the oscillations to be more pronounced
when the wings beat in-phase ($\sigma_0 = 0^\circ$) than
when they beat out-of-phase ($\sigma_0 = 180^\circ$).
In fact, if the forewing and hindwing motions are identical
except for the phase shift, the wing inertia effect will be zero
(true for translation, not for body pitch, which we do not consider
in the present model).

We introduce a relative wing mass parameter $m_w^* = m_w / m$.
According to \cite{wakeling1997}, values range from 0.01 to 0.03 for \emph{Anisoptera}.
We run one hover wingbeat to obtain the body oscillation peak-to-peak amplitude,
comparing the ideal massless wing case to the $m_w^* = 0.02$ case,
for the reference configuration of \autoref{tab:ref_config} (\autoref{fig:oscillations}).
The wing mass and aerodynamic contributions to the
body oscillations appear to be of the same order,
except, as expected, when the wings beat out-of-phase.
We will consider $m_w^* = 0$ in the following, but
should keep in mind that this is only effectively true
at $\sigma_0 = 180^\circ$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{../figures/inertia_vs_aero_sigma0.light.png}
    \caption{Effect of $\sigma_0$ and $m_w^*$ on body oscillations during hover.}\label{fig:oscillations}
\end{figure}

Another effect of the aerodynamic force oscillations is positional drift.
For a constant, open-loop wingbeat pattern, the dragonfly settles
in a steady-state that has a small but nonzero mean body velocity,
when the equilibrium prediction is that the zero mean force condition
occurs at exactly zero mean body velocity.

Overall these effects are relatively mild, with instantaneous body velocities
of approximately $0.25 s_0^* \omega^*$ in the worst case scenario ($\sigma_0 = 0^\circ$,
$\omega^* = 8$, $A^* / m^* = 1.0$), and only about $\frac{1}{10}$ of that
for $\sigma_0 = 180^\circ$, so the conclusions drawn from the equilibrium
analysis should be applicable to controller design.

\subsubsection{Proposed Control Scheme}

Recall we identified $\phi_1$ and $\psi_0$ as the two control
variables for hover. $\phi_1$ controls force magnitude, and
$\psi_0$ controls force direction, with the secondary effect
of reducing the force magnitude as it moves away from the stroke
plane normal mean $\psi_0 = 0$. It seems natural to use $\phi_1$
to control altitude, and $\psi_0$ to control fore-aft drift
by tilting the force direction vector from the vertical.
Specifically we propose the following control scheme
\begin{align*}
    \psi_0 &= \psi_0^\text{eq} - K_x (x - x_0) \\
    \phi_1 &= \phi_1^\text{eq} - K_z (z - z_0)
\end{align*}
Note that orienting the force in the $-x$ direction requires
pitching up (increasing $\psi_0$), so we will need $K_x < 0$.
To design the proportional gains for the reference configuration
(\autoref{tab:ref_config}) we begin by forming the Jacobian matrix
of the wingbeat-averaged aerodynamic force $\vec{F}_a^*$
at the hover equilibrium, using finite differences,
to evaluate the control effectiveness of the two variables
about the force axes. The Jacobian is
\begin{align*}
    J = \begin{bmatrix}
        \displaystyle \frac{\partial F^*_{a,x}}{\partial \psi_0} &\displaystyle  \frac{\partial F^*_{a,x}}{\partial \phi_1} \\
        \displaystyle  \frac{\partial F^*_{a,z}}{\partial \psi_0} &  \displaystyle \frac{\partial F^*_{a,z}}{\partial \phi_1}
        \end{bmatrix}
        \approx \begin{bmatrix}
            -2.06 & -0.05 \\
            +0.93 & 5.83
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{align*}
Notably $\phi_1$ has a negligible effect on force tilt
from the vertical ($F^*_{a,x}$), while as we pointed out before $\psi_0$
does have a significant secondary effect on $F^*_{a,z}$.
Since $\phi_1$ cleanly drives $F^*_{a,z}$, its control
loop will absorb the $\psi_0$ cross term.
The decoupled dynamics are (note mass matrix is $I$ because of
nondimensionalization)
\begin{align*}
    \ddot{x}^* &= -2.06 \times \delta \psi_0 \\
    \ddot{z}^* &= 5.83 \times \delta \phi_1
\end{align*}
So $\omega_n^2 = -2.06 K_x = 5.83 K_z$. We want $\omega_n$
slow enough that the controller sees the wingbeat-averaged
force and not the fluctuations. Using $\omega_n = 1.5$ (10
times smaller than the reference wingbeat frequency $\omega^* = 14$)
we get
\begin{align*}
    K_x &= -1.09 \\
    K_z &= 0.39
\end{align*}

\subsection{Generalized Controller}\label{sec:general-controller}

\subsubsection{Design Principle}

Proposal to extend hover controller to moving/maneuvering flight

\begin{itemize}
    \item Similar inner loop, with $\phi_1$ controlling magnitude and $\psi_0$ controlling direction
    \item $\delta_0$ still fixed at $90^\circ$
    \item $\psi_1$ becomes slaved to advance ratio, always giving maximum $C_{F^*}$
    \item $\gamma$ becomes slaved to flight direction and advance ratio. At zero advance ratio, it is set to the hover value (fixed parameter, $\gamma_h = -40^\circ$). In the high limit, it is set to $\gamma_f$ such that $\vec{n}$ points in the body velocity direction. In between, $\gamma$ is set to $f(J) \gamma_f + (1 - f(J)) \gamma_h$, with $f(J)$ between 0 and 1, $f'(J) \geq 0$, $f(0) = 0$, $f(\infty) = 1$.
    \item Because $\gamma_h \neq 0$, there is an asymmetry in this basic formulation between flight along $+x$ and along $-x$. We can resolve this by flipping the sign of $\gamma_h$ depending on the desired direction of motion.
\end{itemize}

The controller should take into account the
inclined hover stroke plane, and transition
smoothly between and hoving and steady motion.
The in-plane body velocity component should
remain small compared to the wing velocity relative to the body.

\subsubsection{Proposed Control Scheme}

We concretize the principle above as a velocity-scheduled controller. The
pitch phase is held at $\delta_0 = 90^\circ$. The stroke-plane angle follows
\begin{align}
    \gamma(J) = f(J)\,\gamma_f + \bigl(1 - f(J)\bigr)\,\gamma_h, \qquad
    f(J) = 1 - e^{-(J/J_c)^2},
\end{align}
where $\gamma_f = \operatorname{atan2}(-\hat v_x, \hat v_z)$ aligns the
stroke-plane normal $\vec{n}$ with the body velocity $\hat{\vec v}$ (axial
inflow, $\chi \to 0$), $\gamma_h = -40^\circ\,\operatorname{sign}(\hat v_x)$
is the inclined hover plane leaning toward the commanded direction, and the
blend $f$ is monotone with $f(0) = 0$, $f(\infty) = 1$ (we use
$J_c = 0.35$). The pitch amplitude $\psi_1$ is slaved to the advance ratio
at the force-maximizing value $\psi_1^{\max}(J)$ of \autoref{sec:spn-force}
(\autoref{fig:axial_cpsi}). The two hover handles then remain: $\phi_1$ for
magnitude and $\psi_0$ for direction, trimmed to the required force by the
same $2\times2$ inversion as the hover inner loop, evaluated at the current
body velocity. With no body drag, steady straight flight needs the
weight-up force at every speed, so this schedule is the equilibrium trim and
maneuvering is the transient excursion of $(\phi_1, \psi_0)$ away from it.

\subsubsection{Results}

We trim the proposed controller to weight-up along forward, climb, and
descent velocity rays, and compare it to a baseline that holds the stroke
plane horizontal ($\gamma = 0$) and re-optimizes $\psi_1$ for power.

In forward flight the schedule tilts the stroke plane toward vertical so the
wing meets the inflow axially, and the cycle-mean force sits near the
vertical with little redirection. The mean pitch $\psi_0$ \emph{decreases}
with speed, from its $27^\circ$ hover value to $5^\circ$ at $J = 1.0$,
staying well clear of its bound; $\phi_1$ stays small; and the aerodynamic
power is nearly flat ($\langle P^* \rangle \approx 1.0$--$1.4$;
\autoref{fig:gen_compare}). The horizontal baseline meets the same
flight as in-plane wind and holds the force vertical only by driving
$\psi_0$ toward its limit ($-57^\circ$ at $J = 0.8$) and flapping harder, at
a steeply rising cost ($\langle P^* \rangle \approx 7.4$ at $J = 0.8$, roughly five
times the proposed controller).

In vertical flight the ranking reverses: climb and descent are already axial
at $\gamma = 0$, so the baseline trims them at $\psi_0 = 0$ and low power
while the proposed schedule carries the inclined hover plane into low-speed
vertical flight at a modest power penalty (\autoref{fig:gen_compare}). Forward flight and climb remain
feasible beyond $J = 1$ for both controllers; descent limits the proposed
controller to $J \approx 0.35$, where the over-supporting inflow exceeds the
weight at the minimum flapping amplitude.

A more demanding test reverses the body velocity in both axes: the controller
is commanded around a closed circular flight path---forward, climb, backward,
descent, and back to rest---so that $v_x$ and $v_z$ each change sign twice
(\autoref{fig:gen_loop}), tracked with a peak velocity error of $0.05$. The
loop is deliberately kept gentle (advance ratio $J \lesssim 0.16$): a faster
loop fails at the bottom of the circle, where the body descends as $v_x$
reverses, so the axial-descent geometry and the $\pm x$ stroke-plane sign flip
coincide, $\psi_0$ saturates at its bound, and the body drops out of the
loop---the controller's limit in dynamic descent.

The same velocity tracker drives the prey-capture case studied in the project
documentation. We close the loop with pure-pursuit guidance: at each control
update the commanded velocity is aimed along the line of sight to the prey, at
a fixed speed exceeding the prey's. Starting from rest, the dragonfly closes
the range monotonically and intercepts a horizontally moving target within
$0.5\,L$ (\autoref{fig:gen_pursuit}). A descending target would instead inherit
the dynamic-descent limit noted above.
